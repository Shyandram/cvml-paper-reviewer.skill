# Task Neighbors: Similar Fields per Task

Each task has its own neighboring fields: the fields whose reviewers a venue also assigns, whose methods the paper may borrow, and where its "new" idea may already exist under another name. The full-mode ledger review uses this file in three places:

- **Step 0.** Identify the paper's task or tasks, then read their rows.
- **Step 1.** Check novelty against the neighbors as well as the task itself. A mechanism new to the task may be standard in a neighbor. A transfer is still a contribution, but only if the paper says it is one and shows what the transfer required.
- **Step 3.** Draw the roster's outside types from the task's neighbors, not from a generic "adjacent field".

**These rows are rough starting points, not a closed list.** Generate the neighbors, reviewer backgrounds and objections for the paper in front of you, from its own methods, data, evaluation and citations. Use the table only to check that nothing obvious is missing. Add fields that are not listed whenever the paper draws on them, such as a hardware field for a sensor paper or law for a data-licensing paper, and drop listed ones that do not apply. For a task not listed, generate the row yourself; the closest row can help. Write the neighbors you used in the review header, and mark those you added. The rows describe **lines of work**, not specific papers. Name a specific prior work only under Hard Rule 2, or after a literature query finds it.

Each row has four columns:

- **Neighboring fields:** fields that share data, methods or evaluation with the task.
- **Reviewer backgrounds a venue assigns:** the backgrounds that commonly show up on this task's papers, besides specialists in the task itself.
- **Where cross-field prior art hides:** the mechanisms a paper in this task often presents as new that a neighbor already has.
- **What neighbors question:** the objections reviewers from those fields typically raise.

## Using this file in a review

1. **Name the task and its neighbors** in the review header. A paper can sit on two rows: for example, 3D generation draws on both "3D vision" and "image and video generation". Then take the union of their neighbors, weighted toward the row the paper's main claim belongs to.
2. **Novelty (Step 1).** For each central claim, ask in which neighbor the mechanism could already exist. Search there too, by concept words and in the neighbor's vocabulary (e.g. "bundle adjustment" rather than "pose refinement"). Write cross-field prior art as a `judgment` reject reason that names the neighbor and the work. A disclosed and argued transfer is an accept reason, not a reject reason.
3. **Roster (Step 3).** The task's own specialists speak for the task's core aspects. Draw the outside types from the task's neighbors, in the order of the second column: the first background listed is the one a venue assigns most often. Give each outside type the objections in the last column as its "Distrusts".
