# Peer Review Report

## Paper: Localized Cultural Knowledge is Conserved and Controllable in Large Language Models
## Reviewer: Daniyar Sultanbekov, Reviewer 1 (Methodology) (industrial research lab; fictional persona)
## Expertise: steering and probing protocols for LLMs; LLM-evaluation statistics; benchmark construction
## Date: 2026-11-10

## Summary
The paper asks whether LLMs that default to English-centric answers in non-English prompts still hold local cultural knowledge. It defines an "explicit–implicit localization gap" as the accuracy difference between a prompt that states the user's country and a translated prompt that does not (Sections 2.2 and 2.4). It measures this on a small synthetic benchmark plus CulturalBench across seven models, and finds large gaps, especially for smaller models. It then shows that explicit prompting slightly homogenises open-ended generations. Activation patching on Gemma2-9b-it points to layers 23–30. A contrastive steering vector built from "I live in [X]" versus no context recovers part of the gap, transfers across tasks and languages, and is claimed to keep diversity and reduce stereotypes (Tables 2–4, 7, 8).

## Strengths
### S1: A clear, deployment-relevant question
Separating what a model can do when told the culture from what it does when only the language signals it (Figure 1, Section 1) is a useful distinction for how evaluations are read. The 2×2 design is simple and easy to follow.
### S2: Reasonable breadth and several good controls
Seven models, five languages and roughly 12k items (Table 5, which I re-added: all row and column totals check). Two-pass option-order averaging (Section 2.2). An open analysis of GPT-4o refusals (Table 9). The five-option multiple-choice control (Appendix E) directly answers the "steering may just learn the non-American answer" worry.
### S3: A coherent patching → steering → transfer pipeline
Figure 4 (patching), Figure 5 (steering across layers and α), Table 2 (cross-task transfer) and Table 4 (held-out-culture vector) form a logical chain, and the sign test (α = −2 lowers localization, Section 3.3) is a useful dose-response check.
### S4: Honest limitations
The Limitations section concedes that the universal vector is not rigorously evaluated and that no user study was done.

## Weaknesses

### W1 (MAJOR): Steering vectors and (layer, α) are selected and evaluated on the same data; no held-out split is stated
Protocol sweep (full text, appendix included, for split / validation / held-out / select / best-performing / sweep): the only statements about selection are in Appendix D and E.1. Appendix D says "The best parameter is determined by the task the steering vector was defined to solve", with each vector defined as "Best performance on localizing the translated prompts with no localization for each culture". Table 10 is captioned "Best-performing steering configuration per culture across all steering vectors", and Table 13 says "Optimal steering parameters for the multiple choice question answering task". The vectors are built from all four tasks (names, cities, o1-distilled, CulturalBench) (Section 3.3), and no disjoint extraction/evaluation split is described. The benchmark has no validation split, so every row in Tables 2, 4, 11 and 12 is both the data the configuration was chosen on and the data it is reported on. This applies even to the "held-out" universal vector. Only the vector is held out. Its layer and α are chosen per target culture (Table 10, `vuniversal (tr.)`; for the United States α = −2). The partial out-of-sample evidence that exists is cross-task transfer (Table 2) and English-to-translated transfer (Figure 5, bottom). Those help, but they do not give an unbiased estimate of the headline recovery numbers.
- **Suggested Fix**: Split every dataset by question (and by name pair) into a dev half and a test half. Choose layer and α on dev, report test with bootstrap CIs, and rerun the held-out universal vector with α and layer fixed from the other cultures. Cost: well under 20 GPU-hours on a 9B model (about 7 layers × 5 α values × ~12k prompts of forward passes).

### W2 (MAJOR): The gap confounds language with context and is defined inconsistently across sections
Section 2.4 and Figure 2's caption compare "English with cultural context" against "local language with no context", so two factors change at once. Figure 1B describes a full 2×2 grid, but the two off-diagonal cells (translated + context, English + no context) are not reported, except implicitly in the United-States row. "Explicit" means English + context in Section 2.4 and Figure 2, but Section 3.2 says "For explicit localization, we use only translated" examples and Table 4's "Explicit" column is evidently on translated prompts. The model averages quoted in Section 3.1 (35%, 22%, 37%, 20%) include the United States row, where the "implicit" condition is plain English. I recomputed them from the Figure 2 values in the extracted text: Gemma2-9b (0.27+0.34+0.39+0.38+0.35)/5 = 0.346, Gemma2-27b 0.22, Llama-8b-it 0.368, Llama-70b-it 0.198. The US-row gaps (up to 0.43 for Llama-70b) are as large as the non-English ones. So part of the "gap" is how much "I live in the United States" shifts an English default. It is not a multilingual localization failure, which the paper itself half-notes ("this is a consequence of the universality of English"). Excluding the US row, Llama-70b falls to 0.14.
- **Suggested Fix**: Report all four cells of the 2×2 per model and language. Decompose into a language effect and a context effect. Define the gap equation (Section 2.4) in terms of accuracy, as reported. Report averages with and without the US row.

### W3 (MAJOR): Construct validity of the benchmark and unvalidated synthetic data
The Names and Cities items ask "Which of the following names is most likely to be my name?" (Appendix A.2) with one US and one local option. That is answerable only once the country is known, so the explicit prompt reveals the answer variable and the implicit one cannot. On the only pre-existing benchmark, the gap is small: from Table 2, CulturalBench Explicit 0.860 − Implicit 0.752 = 0.108, versus Cities 0.574, Names 0.302 and o1-distilled 0.229 (all derived). The large headline gaps therefore come from the synthetic identity-inference items. Data quality is unaudited: Bangladesh names are generated by GPT-4o ("the website does not contain entries from Bangladesh"), o1-distilled labels come from the generating model, all translations come from GPT-4o ("We translate all prompts using GPT-4o"), and country mentions are stripped from CulturalBench by GPT-4o, which can make items ambiguous. No native-speaker check is reported, which matters most for Bengali, the only low-resource language.
- **Suggested Fix**: Report the gap per dataset in the main text. Have native speakers audit ~100 items per language (translation, label correctness, ambiguity after country removal). Separate "identity inference" items from "knowledge" items.

### W4 (MAJOR): No uncertainty on the main tables; several claimed differences are inside the noise
Decoding is greedy, so each cell is one deterministic run, and Tables 2, 4, 11 and 12 and Figure 2 carry no CIs. Per-culture cells have a few hundred to a few thousand items (Table 5; for example 120 CulturalBench-US items gives SE ≈ 0.03–0.045). The "task-agnostic" claim rests partly on differences that small: in Table 2, CulturalBench `vnames` = 0.769 against Implicit 0.752 is +0.017. Table 11's implicit-steering gains average about 0.079 (derived), against a stated "on average around 7%".
- **Suggested Fix**: Bootstrap CIs over questions for every table, and paired McNemar or bootstrap tests for steered vs. implicit. Post-hoc, negligible cost.

### W5 (MAJOR): Open-ended diversity and stereotype evidence is thin and its metrics are not validated
Table 1 differences are +0.026 and +0.031 (Gemma2), +0.025 and +0.031 (Llama) and +0.007 for GPT-4o (derived), against bootstrap SEs of about 0.006–0.008. The GPT-4o difference is under one standard error of the difference. The sentence "homogeneity goes up (except for Aya" overstates it for GPT-4o. Embedding cosine from `text-embeddings-3-small` is not validated as a diversity measure, and an explicit prompt that names a country will legitimately make generations cluster around that country, so higher similarity conflates localization with mode collapse. Tables 7 and 8 report quantities that are never defined (what does 0.011 mean for "Implicit" stereotypicality?) with SEs up to ±0.10. The judge is GPT-4o with no human agreement check, and the Figure 12 prompt contains "they are of culture culture", which suggests an unfilled template slot. Steering results here are for Gemma2-9b-it only.
- **Suggested Fix**: Define the Table 7/8 metric. Validate the judge on ~100 pairs per language with native speakers. Add a lexical diversity measure and a localization measure next to diversity so the trade-off can be seen on one plot. Add CIs.

### W6 (MAJOR): The mechanistic claims rest on one model and have no control task
Patching (Figure 4, Appendix F) is run on Gemma2-9b-it only. The curves (target answer early, source answer late, a middle spike) are the generic shape expected from last-token residual patching of any information. There is no control attribute (for example gender of a name, or a topic) to show that the layer range "namely 23 and 30" is specific to culture. The conclusion that the mechanisms "may be universal" is drawn from similar curves across cultures and tasks (Appendix F), which is not a test of universality across models or attributes.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add a non-cultural contrast pair as a control and replicate on a second model family (for example Llama-3.1-8b-it). About 10–20 GPU-hours.

### W7: Reproducibility gaps
Not stated after search: compute, seeds (moot for greedy decoding, but not for open-ended sampling), GPT-4o/o1/judge model versions and API dates, number of examples used to build each steering vector, and exactly where the vector is added (Section 2.3 writes the last token; the Discussion says the vector is added only for newly generated tokens). A code and data link is mentioned, but the block is unreadable here, so this is unverified, not missing.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add a short reproducibility table (versions, dates, vector sample counts, injection positions).

**Minor**: "steretypical", "insufficent", "Bengladesh", "Gemma2-9b-is", figure numbering out of order (Figure 14 before Figures 12–13), the Table 2 caption does not name the model.

# Peer Review Report

## Paper: Localized Cultural Knowledge is Conserved and Controllable in Large Language Models
## Reviewer: Lucía Montenegro-Aoki, Reviewer 2 (Domain) (multilingual NLP group, public university, Latin America; fictional persona)
## Expertise: cultural-alignment benchmarks; multilingual representation analysis
## Date: 2026-11-10

## Summary
The paper contrasts "explicit" cultural prompting with "implicit" language-only cues, quantifies a gap on a new four-dataset benchmark, and then uses patching and contrastive activation addition to build a vector that localizes outputs without stating the culture. It positions itself against English-translated multilingual benchmarks, cultural benchmarks that evaluate in the explicit setting, and mechanistic work on language-agnostic concept representations.

## Strengths
### S1: A well-chosen conceptual distinction
Section 4 explains that existing cultural benchmarks (Chiu et al.; Naous et al.; Singh et al.) correspond to the explicit setting, and Section 5 argues that "explicit" measures capability while "implicit" is closer to ecological validity. This is a real contribution to how cultural evaluations are read.
### S2: Good connection to the authors' own mechanistic line
The patching setup (Section 2.3) follows Wendler et al. 2024 and Dumas et al. 2025 and extends it from concepts to cultural answers. The documented departure from CAA's pairing (Section 2.3: pairing translated vs. English and with vs. without context) is explained.
### S3: Timely and relevant
Cultural customization of LLMs is an active topic, and a training-free control at inference time matches interest in steering.
### S4: A balanced discussion
Section 5 acknowledges what steering did not do (steering "did not recover the full cultural" localization performance of adding explicit context).

## Weaknesses

### W1 (MAJOR): The novelty and "first" claim are overstated
Section 4 says "our work is the first that attempts to" rigorously measure the explicit–implicit performance difference. Tao et al. (2024, PNAS Nexus), which the paper cites, already compares default prompting with "cultural prompting" across GPT models. Zhong et al. 2024, "Cultural Value Differences of LLMs: Prompt, Language, and Model Size" (uncited; verified), studies how prompting language and prompt variation change expressed cultural values. Romanou et al. (cited) evaluate in the local-language setting. The delta is real but narrower: a controlled 2×2 on identity-inference items plus a gap metric. Per-claim novelty: C1 medium (prior coverage about level 3), C2 high (no close prior work found in this search), C3 medium, C4 incremental to medium (below), C5 high but unproven.
- **Suggested Fix**: Replace "first … rigorously" with a precise delta against Tao et al. and Zhong et al.

### W2 (MAJOR): Prior work on representation-level cultural steering is not discussed or compared
The paper motivates steering as an alternative to cultural prompting. Dai et al., "From Word to World: Evaluate and Mitigate Culture Bias in LLMs via Word Association Test" (EMNLP 2025; verified, prior (peer-reviewed), May 2025), proposes CultureSteer, which per its abstract "moves beyond superficial cultural prompting by embedding cultural-specific semantic associations directly within the model's internal representations". The motivation is the same as the paper's. It is not cited. Masoud et al., "Cultural Alignment in Large Language Models Using Soft Prompt Tuning" (arXiv 2025; verified, arXiv-only more than 6 months before cutoff), is a second inference-time or lightweight alternative. Steering is compared only to one explicit template ("I live in [X]", Section 2.2). There is no richer cultural prompt (such as Tao et al.'s), few-shot prompt, persona/system prompt or soft-prompt baseline. Under the literature rules this is a MAJOR gap at most, not a reason on its own to reject: I judge from the abstracts only.
- **Suggested Fix**: Discuss both works in Section 4, and add at least one stronger prompting baseline (a persona/system prompt plus a few-shot prompt), which is cheap.

### W3 (MAJOR): "A culture vector" may be a country-identity or "non-American" axis
CAA is an existing method (Panickssery et al.), so the contribution is the contrast design and its application. The abstract's description of an explicit cultural customization vector conserved across all non-English languages explored claims more than the evidence. Binary items always include a US option (Section 3.3: "setting, the cultural steering vector may simply" learn the non-American answer). The universal vector for the US needs α = −2 (Table 10, `vuniversal (tr.)`, United States), which is what a "foreignness" direction would predict. Appendix E's five-option control recovers only part of the gap: from Table 12, `vtr.` closes 68% (Bangladesh), 12% (France), 41% (Russia), 43% (Turkey) and 60% (US) of the implicit-to-explicit gap, and `vuniversal` closes 27%, 9%, 44%, 28% and 24% (all derived). France is 0.292 against Implicit 0.233.

# Peer Review Report

## Paper: Localized Cultural Knowledge is Conserved and Controllable in Large Language Models
## Reviewer: Samuel Achterberg-Nwosu, Reviewer 3 (Perspective) (computational social science and cultural anthropology department, North America; fictional persona)
## Expertise: language and identity in sociolinguistics; cultural psychology; personalization
## Date: 2026-11-10

## Summary
The paper opens with a bilingual-cognition anecdote (Japanese speakers saying pumpkins are green) and shows an inverse pattern in LLMs: asked in Japanese, GPT-4o says orange, and asked about Japan, it says green. It builds a gap measure and a steering vector that moves a model toward the culture implied by the prompt's language without stating it. It argues that this gives a "soft control" path for customization and a warning for how cultural benchmarks are interpreted.

## Strengths
### S1: The ecological-validity framing is valuable
Section 5 contrasts evaluations for model developers (what the model can do when told) with evaluations for downstream users (what happens with ordinary prompts), citing Brunswik and Kapoor et al. That insight transfers to other areas, such as safety or personalization evaluations.
### S2: Steering as personalization
A training-free, per-user control that does not require the user to say "I live in X" has appeal, for example for users who do not wish to volunteer identity. Figure 15 (a Texas chili recipe instead of a Tuscan one) shows the idea concretely.
### S3: Cross-lingual transfer is encouraging
That a vector built from English pairs raises localization on translated prompts (Figure 5, bottom; caption description only) suggests shared structure and invites follow-up on bilingual and multicultural users.
### S4: Candour about scope
The Limitations section admits there is no user study, no reasoning models and only five languages.

## Weaknesses (opportunities)

### W1: The key assumption, language ⇒ nationality ⇒ culture, deserves a bolder treatment
The paper treats the prompt language as a proxy for one country (Section 2) and counts the other country's answer as an error. In sociolinguistics, language choice also signals register, migration history and audience, and bicultural speakers shift cultural frames with the language of the situation (the "cultural frame switching" line of work in cultural psychology). The authors' own surprise that English implicit prompts do poorly ("this is a consequence of the universality of English", Section 3.1) shows the assumption straining: English users are not all American. More importantly, inferring a user's nationality from the language of their prompt is itself a stereotyping act and a privacy-sensitive inference. GPT-4o's refusals (64% of city questions, Table 9) are described as cautious behavior, but they can equally be read as the intended behavior when identity is unspecified.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add a scoping subsection on language vs. culture; consider "refuse or ask" as a correct response in the implicit condition and score it separately.

### W2: Deployment and risk of per-country vectors
Steering needs white-box access, so it is not available for GPT-4o, the model of the opening example. For open models, a vector per country makes it easy to ship coarse national stereotypes. The qualitative samples in Figures 17 and 18 show how thin the target can be: the Turkey vector produces "a traditional dance from the Levant region of the Middle East" and "Muhammara - Syrian Red Pepper and Walnut Dip", and the Bangladesh vector produces "a classical dance form from" Tamil Nadu. The faithfulness claim rests on a GPT-4o judge with no native-speaker check (Appendix B.3), which the Limitations section itself flags.
- **Suggested Fix**: A small human evaluation with native speakers (even 50 pairs per language), and a short discussion of national-level granularity and within-country diversity.

### W3: Scope of cultures and languages
Five countries with large web footprints, one low-resource language (Bengali) and no Indigenous or African languages. The abstract's claim of a vector conserved across all non-English languages explored should be read as five languages.
- **Suggested Fix**: Say "five languages" in the abstract; add one low-resource, non-national-language case if feasible.

### W4: Directions that would make the work stronger
Mixing vectors with weights (α) for multicultural identities, user-profile-conditioned steering, comparison with fine-tuning or preference-based cultural adaptation, and a multi-turn user study. The results on 5-way choice (Appendix E) point towards compositional use.
- **Suggested Fix**: One of these, in the discussion or as a small experiment.

**Minor**: the pumpkin example would be stronger with a count over many concepts instead of a single anecdote (Figures 6–7).

*(Reviewer 3: no specific papers named; lines of work only.)* Cultural frame switching in bicultural individuals (cultural psychology); research on identity inference from language in personalization and user modelling.

# Devil's Advocate Report

## Paper: Localized Cultural Knowledge is Conserved and Controllable in Large Language Models
## Reviewer: Rahul Teixeira-Brandt, Devil's Advocate (evaluation-methodology group, research institute, Western Europe; fictional persona)

---

## Steelman
Language-only prompts leave much cultural knowledge unused: simply saying "I live in Turkey" changes answers a lot, and a single vector extracted from that contrast can move a 9B model part of the way without saying anything in the prompt. The effect transfers across languages and tasks, which is what one would expect if a shared, controllable representation exists, and the open-ended results suggest it does so without the homogenisation of prompting.

## Core Argument Chain
1. P1: Accuracy with a stated country is much higher than with the translated language-only prompt (Figure 2, Tables 2 and 4).
2. P2: So the model has the knowledge but does not use it by default (the abstract: "it may not naturally surface").
3. P3: Explicit prompting reduces diversity and increases stereotypes (Table 1, Figure 14).
4. P4: Patching shows implicit and explicit localization act in the same layers of Gemma2-9b-it (Figure 4).
5. P5: A vector from the explicit-vs-control contrast adds localization, is task- and language-agnostic, keeps diversity and reduces stereotypes (Tables 2–4, 7, 8).
6. ∴ Localized cultural knowledge is conserved and controllable, and a universal culture vector exists.

## Strongest Counter-Argument
The "gap" is mostly an information gap, not evidence of latent knowledge that fails to surface. The explicit prompt hands the model the decisive variable (the country), and the implicit prompt does not. The Names and Cities items ("Which of the following names is most likely to be my name?", Appendix A.2) are answerable only given that variable, and the prompt language is a weak proxy for it, as the paper itself concedes. A calibrated model should be unsure in the implicit condition, and on the one pre-existing benchmark, where the gap is small (Table 2, CulturalBench 0.752 vs 0.860), the headline effect nearly disappears. GPT-4o's refusal of 64% of city questions (Table 9) is, on this reading, the right behavior.

The steering result inherits the same problem. The vector is the difference between "I live in X" and nothing, in a design where one option is always American (Section 3.3). It can therefore encode "the user stated a foreign country" or "pick the non-American option". The paper's own control hints at this: the universal vector for the United States needs α = −2 (Table 10), and in the five-option control, `vtr.` closes only 12% of the gap for France (Table 12, 0.308 vs Implicit 0.233 vs Explicit 0.860; derived). Layer and α are then chosen per culture on the same data that are reported (Tables 10 and 13). So what the evidence supports is that a one-axis, country-identity control exists in one 9B model, partly recovering a gap that is itself partly an information gap. It does not support "localized cultural knowledge is conserved", which the title asserts for LLMs in general. The diversity and stereotype argument cannot rescue it: the implicit condition looks "diverse and unstereotyped" because its outputs are not localized at all (Figure 15 implicit: Tuscan Ribollita; Figure 16 implicit: "Global Story Sharing Day").

## Editorial Decision Letter

Dear Authors,

Thank you for submitting "Localized Cultural Knowledge is Conserved and Controllable in Large Language Models" to ICLR.

The paper addresses a timely question: whether LLMs that answer non-English prompts with English-centric content still hold local cultural knowledge that can be switched on without naming the culture. The explicit/implicit framing and the patching-to-steering pipeline are creative and well motivated.

**Overall Assessment:** The reviewers found the question and framing valuable and the controls in the appendix thoughtful. They disagreed on how far the evidence supports the title: two reviewers leaned toward acceptance for the framing and the cross-language transfer, and three leaned toward rejection because the central measurement is confounded and the steering results are selected and reported on the same data. My assessment is that a careful panel would likely place this draft just below the bar in its current form.

**Key Strengths Identified by Reviewers:**
1. A clear, reusable distinction between implicit and explicit cultural evaluation (R2, R3, R4).
2. A coherent patching → steering → transfer pipeline, with useful controls (option order, five-option choice, implicit steering) (R1, R4).
3. Honest limitations, and a steering vector that moves outputs toward the target culture in five languages (R1, R3, R4).

**Key Concerns Requiring Attention:**
1. The steering configuration (layer, α) and the vectors are tuned and reported on the same data, with no held-out split (R1, DA; CONSENSUS-2).
2. The gap conflates language and context, is defined differently across sections, and is large mainly on synthetic identity-inference items; on CulturalBench it is 0.108 (R1, R2, R3, DA; CONSENSUS-4).
3. The open-ended diversity, stereotype and faithfulness claims are partly inside noise or anecdotal, and the mechanism and steering rest on one 9B model (R1, R3, R4, DA; CONSENSUS-4/3).
4. The novelty claim is overstated and the closest steering work is not discussed or compared (R2, R4, DA; CONSENSUS-3).

**Decision: Borderline Reject (3)**

What would change the decision: a dev/test protocol with confidence intervals; the full 2×2 and per-dataset gaps; specificity controls (cross-culture, random and non-American vectors); a second model; a human check of the judge; and a title and abstract that state the recovery fraction and the scope. Please refer to the Revision Roadmap below.

Sincerely,
Ingrid Halvorsen-Mbeki
Editor-in-Chief (meta-reviewer)
