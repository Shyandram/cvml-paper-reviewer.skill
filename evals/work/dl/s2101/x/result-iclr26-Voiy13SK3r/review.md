# Peer Review Report

## Paper: Helmsman: Autonomous Synthesis of Federated Learning Systems via Collaborative LLM Agents
## Reviewer: Dr. Henrik Valtonen-Reyes, Reviewer 1 (Methodology) (industrial research lab, Northern Europe; fictional persona)
## Expertise: federated optimization benchmarks; non-IID evaluation protocols; LLM-agent evaluation
## Date: 2026-11-10

## Summary
The paper presents a three-stage multi-agent LLM pipeline (human-approved planning, supervised modular code generation in Flower, and a simulation-based debug loop) that turns a templated natural-language query into an FL codebase, and a 16-task benchmark (AgentFL-Bench) built from such queries. The authors report accuracy of the generated solutions against FedAvg, FedProx and one "specialized" method per task (Tables 2–4), plus a detailed Split-CIFAR100 comparison (Table 5) and a figure of debugging attempts by intervention level (Fig. 4).

## Protocol sweep (Step 0)
Searched the full text including the appendix for split/selection statements. Findings: per-client "80/20 train/test split with seed=42" (App. A.4.2); server evaluation "use partition 0 for server evaluation" (App. A.4.5); the evaluator's heuristics use simulation logs "for a small number of federated rounds (N = 5)" (§3.3); human verification of final code for complex tasks (§6, "concludes with human verification of the final synthesized code"). **Not stated after search:** any validation split; which run or round supplies the numbers in Tables 2–4; whether the human reviewer or the L2 check sees test accuracy; baseline hyperparameters and tuning budget; seeds for Tables 2–4.

## Strengths

### S1: A concrete, well-scoped system with disclosed prompts
§3 and Appendix A.3–A.5 give the full role prompts, the module decomposition (Task/Client/Strategy/Server), the dependency order and the evaluator success criteria. This is unusually transparent for an agent paper and makes the pipeline re-implementable in principle.

### S2: A benchmark that spans several FL sub-areas with explicit queries
Tables 6–8 list all 16 queries, grouped into five areas (heterogeneity, communication, personalization, active, continual learning), and Tables 9–10 give datasets, models and client settings. Having every task as a fixed natural-language query is a useful basis for comparing agentic systems.

### S3: Honest statement of limited autonomy and cost
§6 ("Computational Cost and the Limits of Autonomy") and Figure 4's caption admit that only 62.5% of tasks were fully automatic and that cost bounded the debug budget. Table 5 additionally reports forgetting and a 10-task setting for the headline task (Q16).

## Weaknesses

### W1: The central mechanism (multi-agent planning/coding/evaluation) is never isolated
There is no ablation of any component (self-reflection agent §3.1, HITL approval §3.1, modular teams §3.2, the evaluator/debugger loop §3.3, RAG/web tools §3.4) and no baseline agent: not a single-agent LLM given the same query and tools, nor an existing multi-agent code-generation framework. §5 therefore cannot say whether the results come from the architecture, from the underlying frontier LLM's knowledge of standard FL recipes, or from human intervention. Claim C1 ("three collaborative phases") is described, not tested.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add (i) single-agent Claude-Sonnet-4 with the same Flower scaffold and 10-attempt budget, (ii) leave-one-out ablations (no reflection, no HITL, no modular split, no L2 check), reported as success rate and final accuracy on at least the 16 tasks. Cost: roughly 16 tasks × 4 variants × 3 generations ≈ 200 generation runs plus 100-round simulations; a few GPU-days.

### W2: Mixed autonomy: reported numbers combine fully automatic and human-assisted results without a per-task disclosure
§6 states that complex queries "such as Q16 initiate a structured human-in-the-loop protocol" with "human verification of the final synthesized code", and reports "full automation on 62.5% of benchmark tasks" (= 10 of 16). Tables 2–4 do not mark which of the 16 rows used which intervention level (Fig. 4 distinguishes "No Intervention", "Planning", "Planning + Coding"), how many human messages were exchanged, or whether humans saw test accuracy. The baselines received no comparable human effort. The word "Autonomous" in the title and abstract is therefore only supported for the subset in Fig. 4 labeled No Intervention.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add an intervention-level column to Tables 2–4, report results separately for the fully automatic subset, release the human messages, and report human time per task.

### W3: Baseline comparison is under-specified and not demonstrably fair
§4.2 states baselines are run by "substituting our generated strategy with a baseline method while maintaining identical task configurations", but not who implemented them, with which hyperparameters, or with what tuning budget; the generated solution, in contrast, is produced by a literature-reading agent with up to 10 repair attempts. The "specialized" baseline is one method per task and sometimes mismatched: FedNova is used for communication-efficiency tasks Q10/Q11 (Table 3) although the bibliography entry describes it as addressing objective inconsistency from heterogeneous local updates, and §2.2 and §4.2 describe it differently ("system heterogeneity" vs "distribution shifts"). In Q6, the specialized method (83.08) is below plain FedAvg (84.93), which suggests untuned baselines. For Q16, Table 4 uses FedWeIT (28.56) while Table 5 shows FedLwF (30.94) and TARGET (34.89) scoring higher.
- **Suggested Fix**: Use the strongest standard method per category with a stated, equal tuning budget (e.g., a small learning-rate/μ grid for every method on a validation split), state implementation provenance, and give all baselines the same human-assisted attempt budget as the system.

### W4: No variance, no run counts, and several margins are within plausible noise
Tables 2–4 report single numbers. Relative to the specialized method the margins are +0.47 (Q3), +0.18 (Q7), +0.17 (Q11) and −0.31 (Q12), which are indistinguishable from run-to-run variation in small FL simulations. Moreover, the generated program is itself a random variable (LLM sampling): §6/Fig. 4 mention 3 runs for attempt counts, but Tables 2–4 do not say which generation is reported (first, best, median).
- **Suggested Fix**: Report mean ± std over ≥3 FL training seeds for each method and, separately, over ≥3 independent generations of Helmsman per task (≈ 16 × 3 simulations, modest on small models).

### W5: Evaluation protocol leaves open test-set leakage and mis-measurement
The benchmark has no validation split (App. A.4.2: "80/20 train/test split"), the L2 check and the human verifier read simulation logs that contain accuracy/loss, and the prompt template evaluates the global model with "partition 0" only (App. A.4.5). For non-IID tasks (e.g., Q4, four domain-specific clients) evaluating on one client's held-out slice is not a "global test accuracy". It is not stated how the numbers in Tables 2–4 were computed for Helmsman versus the baselines. I cannot tell whether this is a template artifact or the actual protocol.
- **Suggested Fix**: State the evaluation split for every method, use a separate centralized or union-of-clients test set for global accuracy, and confirm no accuracy-based decision (by the evaluator or a human) touched the reported test split.

### W6: Benchmark definition has internal inconsistencies (see Error Log)
Dataset or model specifications for Q2, Q9, Q10 and Q14 differ between Tables 2/3, 6–8 and 9–10, and the two "specialized" numbers for Q7 and Q8 are identical.
- **Suggested Fix**: Reconcile all tables against the released task files.

### W7: Missing cost and success-rate reporting
Only the average attempts (Fig. 4) are given. There is no token/$ cost, wall-clock, or success rate (fraction of generations that reach L1+L2 SUCCESS within T_max = 10), which are the natural metrics for the "agent" part of the claim.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add a table of success rate, attempts, tokens and cost per task.

**Minor:** §6 heading "DISUCCUSION"; "FedWelt" for FedWeIT in Table 3's caption; Table 2's "79.42∗" marks FedProx with the FedNova symbol; Table 9's caption says global test accuracy for all tasks while Table 10 and A.2 list exceptions (Q12, Q13, Q16).

# Peer Review Report

## Paper: Helmsman: Autonomous Synthesis of Federated Learning Systems via Collaborative LLM Agents
## Reviewer: Prof. Sunita Wickramanayake-Brandt, Reviewer 2 (Domain) (large public university, South Asia; fictional persona)
## Expertise: federated and continual learning; multi-agent LLM code generation
## Date: 2026-11-10

## Summary
The paper applies a planner–coder–tester–debugger multi-agent pattern to the specific domain of federated learning, targeting the Flower framework, and proposes a benchmark of 16 FL tasks. It positions itself as the first systematic application of multi-agent LLM engineering to FL system design (§2.1, "this paradigm has not yet been systematically applied").

## Strengths

### S1: Relevant and under-explored application domain
FL systems have many interacting components (Table 9–10 settings, §2.2 "intractable design space"), and an automated path from a requirement to a runnable Flower project is a sensible target. I found no directly competing LLM-agent system for FL in the literature check (see addendum).

### S2: Related work on multi-agent code generation is accurate for the cited items
§2.1 describes AgentCoder (role separation of implementation and test generation) and CodeSim (simulation-driven planning and debugging) in line with their titles/abstracts, and ties the design to this lineage.

### S3: Q16 analysis is more informative than the rest of the evaluation
Table 5 compares against five continual-learning baselines in two settings (5 and 10 tasks) and reports forgetting, and §6 names the synthesized ingredients (client-side experience replay plus global-model distillation).

## Weaknesses

### W1: The novelty claim ("first"/"not yet systematically applied") rests on an incomplete map of adjacent work
The related work (§2.1) covers code-generation agents and scientific-discovery agents, but omits the closest neighbors: full-pipeline AutoML agents with retrieval-augmented planning and verification, ML-engineering/research-agent benchmarks (the literature check found MLGym, a 2025 benchmark of research tasks for LLM agents), and FL-specific automation (FL benchmarks and hyperparameter/architecture search). The technical pattern (planner + coder + tester + debugger loop with a sandbox) is the same as in the cited AgentCoder/CodeSim/MAGIS family; the novelty is the FL instantiation (Flower module decomposition, FL-specific prompts in App. A.4) and the benchmark. That is a legitimate but incremental contribution and should be stated as such, with a novelty-delta against ML-engineering agents.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add a related-work paragraph and at least one empirical comparison with a generic ML-engineering agent (or an ablation showing FL-specific modules matter).

### W2: Characterization of the single-agent baselines contradicts the bibliography
§1 says systems "like those proposed by Muennighoff et al. (2023) and Dong et al. (2024), often built on single-agent architectures with prompting techniques like Chain-of-Thought … or ReAct, … struggle" with FL-scale systems. The reference list gives Muennighoff et al. as "OctoPack: Instruction tuning code large language models" (a model/dataset paper, not an agent system) and Dong et al. as "Self-collaboration code generation via ChatGPT", whose title indicates a collaborative multi-role design. The "single-agent systems are insufficient" premise, which motivates the multi-agent design, is thus not supported by the cited works, and no single-agent baseline is run (see R1-W1).
- **Suggested Fix**: Cite actual single-agent ML-engineering/code agents and compare against one.

### W3: FL baselines are not the standard strongest for the task categories, which weakens the "competitive with hand-crafted methods" positioning
The specialized baselines are FedNova (Q1, Q4–Q8, Q10–Q11), HeteroFL (Q9), FedPer (Q12–Q13), FAST (Q14–Q15), FedWeIT (Q16) (Tables 2–4). For domain/feature shift (Q4, Q5-Q8), well-known specialized approaches (e.g., local batch-norm methods) are absent; for personalization (Q12–Q13) there are other standard methods; SCAFFOLD and MOON are cited in §2.2 and §1 but never compared. FedNova is not a communication-efficiency or distribution-shift method per its own bibliography title, which makes Q10/Q11 "specialized" rows weak reference points.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add the established method for each category (see Missing References).

### W4: The headline "novel combinatorial strategy" (§6) is a standard recipe
§6 attributes Q16's gain to "client-side experience replay with global model distillation" and calls it novel. Replay-based and distillation-based federated continual learning are established (e.g., FedWeIT is cited; the literature check also surfaced a generative-replay method for continual FL published at ICLR 2023). In Table 5 the strongest baseline, TARGET, is exemplar-free; a replay buffer that stores real data is a different (and easier) resource setting. The paper gives no description of the exact algorithm, so novelty and fairness cannot be judged.
- **Suggested Fix**: Describe the synthesized algorithm, state memory budgets and compare against replay-based FCL baselines.

### W5: Citation accuracy issues
FedEWC is attributed to "Lee et al., 2017" (Table 5), but the bibliography entry is "Overcoming catastrophic forgetting by incremental moment matching" (a different method; EWC is Kirkpatrick et al., 2017). "Luping et al., 2019" in §1 uses the author's given name. Several citations in §1–§2.2 for broad statements (Wu et al., 2022 on communication-efficient distillation; Li et al., 2021 MOON) are only loosely related to the claim of "brittleness in dynamic environments".
- **Suggested Fix**: Correct the citations and match each to its claim.

**Field snapshot (≈150 words; from kept results and general knowledge, before the cutoff).** Three to five years ago FL research was dominated by hand-designed point solutions for individual heterogeneity problems (FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedNova, personalization layers). From 2023 on, LLM agents for software engineering and ML engineering developed rapidly: multi-agent code generation (AgentCoder, MapCoder, CodeSim), issue resolution agents on SWE-bench, and research-agent benchmarks that evaluate end-to-end ML tasks (MLGym in the kept results). For FL specifically, the kept results show LLMs mostly used *inside* FL (federated fine-tuning, in-context agent learning), not agents that *build* FL systems; an open question is whether agents design genuinely new algorithms or reassemble known ones. In this sub-field a significant contribution now would be a controlled demonstration that an agent architecture beats a strong single-agent baseline at matched budgets, or a benchmark with held-out tasks and executable success criteria. A new benchmark with accuracy deltas against FedAvg/FedProx alone is incremental.

# Peer Review Report

## Paper: Helmsman: Autonomous Synthesis of Federated Learning Systems via Collaborative LLM Agents
## Reviewer: Dr. Tomás Albuquerque-Nwosu, Reviewer 3 (Perspective) (software-engineering and human–AI interaction group, university, South America; fictional persona)
## Expertise: AutoML and program synthesis; MLOps; human-in-the-loop design
## Date: 2026-11-10

## Summary
The paper proposes automating the design-implement-test cycle of FL systems with a staged multi-agent LLM pipeline and evaluates it on 16 simulated tasks. Seen from software engineering and AutoML, it is an "ML-system compiler": a specification goes in, a runnable Flower project comes out, with a human approving the plan and sometimes the code.

## Strengths

### S1: A sensible human–AI division of labor
§3.1 argues that human approval of the plan prunes the search space and lowers cost ("resource optimization"), and §6 acknowledges that complex tasks need more human intervention. Putting the human at the plan level rather than at the code level is a design that practitioners can adopt.

### S2: Connects naturally to AutoML and program-synthesis lines of work
The sandbox-in-the-loop repair cycle (§3.3, Hierarchical Diagnosis L1/L2) mirrors execution-feedback loops in program synthesis, and the system could be a platform for AutoML-style search over FL strategy modules. The Strategy module isolation (§3.2) is a good hook for plugging in search.

### S3: Realistic task templates that look like practitioner requests
Table 1 and Tables 6–8 phrase tasks as deployment requests ("I need to deploy … across 15 mobile devices"), which is closer to how non-experts ask than a function signature.

## Weaknesses

### W1: "Deployment-ready" claims are not supported by a simulation-only evaluation
The abstract, §1 and §6 speak of "deployable", "deployment-ready" systems, but all evaluation is in the Flower simulation (§3.4, §4.2) with 4–15 clients and public datasets. No network failures, stragglers, secure aggregation, device resource limits or serialization constraints are tested. Several task names (e.g., Q9 "Resource Constraint", Q10 "Bandwidth Limits") are emulated by configuration (Table 10 limits the device connection rate to 30% for Q10).
- **Suggested Fix**: Tone down to "simulation-validated prototypes", and test one task on a multi-process or emulated-network deployment.

### W2: Privacy and security, the point of FL, are absent from the benchmark and the system's own threat model
The planner template has "Privacy Mechanisms: {privacy} (None if no privacy mechanisms is applied)" (App. A.3.1) and none of the 16 tasks involves differential privacy, secure aggregation or robustness to malicious clients. The design process itself sends task descriptions (e.g., hospital data characteristics, Q7, Q14) to external LLM APIs and a web search tool (§3.4), and executes LLM-written code (§3.3). The paper has no discussion of data leakage through prompts, code-execution safety, or whether generated aggregation code could leak client data.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add a threat-model paragraph, one privacy-constrained task, and a code-safety audit (e.g., static checks on generated client code for raw-data export).

### W3: Assumptions that go unexamined
(i) That the "critical bottleneck" of FL adoption is manual algorithm selection (§1: "This paper argues that this manual design paradigm is the critical bottleneck"); in practice data access, systems integration and governance are often bigger obstacles, and no evidence is given. (ii) That templated queries which already name the dataset, model and partition (Table 1; Tables 6–8) are representative of non-expert requests; under-specified or contradictory requirements are not tested. (iii) That the LLM synthesizes rather than recalls: the tasks use famous datasets and known methods the backbone has likely seen in training, and the Strategy-coder prompt even hard-codes dataset IDs and a UCI-HAR transform (App. A.4.2), so memorization and benchmark-specific scaffolding cannot be separated from design ability.
- **Suggested Fix**: Add held-out tasks with unseen or private datasets and ambiguous queries; report a recall-only control (the LLM asked to name a method without running).

### W4: No user study or human-effort measurement although the central claim is lowering the barrier for non-experts
§1 says Helmsman "dramatically lowers the barrier to entry … for both experts and non-experts". There is no experiment with users, no measure of the human time spent on planning approval or verification (§3.1, §6), and no comparison with the time an expert needs for the same task.
- **Suggested Fix**: A small user study (e.g., 6–10 FL novices and experts, time-to-working-system and final accuracy) or at least logged human interaction counts.

### Cross-disciplinary connections and future directions
Methods worth connecting: automated algorithm discovery with evolutionary program search (cited in §1 as AlphaEvolve), hyperparameter/NAS-style search over the Strategy module, and software-engineering evaluation practice (pass@k with equal budgets, mutation testing of generated code). Natural follow-ups: self-evolution (§7) with a fixed budget, reporting success rate vs. cost curves, and tasks where the right answer is a combination of mechanisms that the LLM could not simply recall.

# Devil's Advocate Report

## Paper: Helmsman: Autonomous Synthesis of Federated Learning Systems via Collaborative LLM Agents
## Reviewer: Dr. Kwabena Osei-Ferreira, Devil's Advocate (independent research institute, North America; fictional persona)

---

## Steelman
LLM agents can already write working FL code; embedding them in a plan–code–simulate–repair loop with Flower as a sandbox, and keeping the human at plan approval, plausibly gives a practical tool for FL researchers. Across 16 templated tasks the generated strategies beat FedAvg/FedProx on 15, match or beat the specialized method on half, and one task (Q16) shows a very large gain with a clear explanation (replay plus distillation).

## Core Argument Chain
1. Manual FL design is the critical bottleneck for FL adoption (§1).
2. Single-agent code generation cannot handle FL's system-level complexity (§1).
3. A three-phase multi-agent pipeline (planning with HITL, modular coding, simulation-based repair) can synthesize complete FL systems (§3).
4. On AgentFL-Bench, the synthesized systems are competitive with and often superior to hand-crafted methods (Tables 2–5).
5. The loop certifies the code as "executable" and "semantically correct" (§3.3).
∴ Helmsman is "a significant step towards the automated engineering of complex decentralized AI systems" (Abstract).

## Strongest Counter-Argument
Even if every number in Tables 2–5 is correct, the experiments cannot distinguish three very different explanations of the results: (a) the three-phase *multi-agent design* works, (b) the *backbone LLM* (Claude-Sonnet-4) already knows how to write a Flower app that applies a textbook remedy (FedProx-style regularization, replay, distillation) to a named problem, and (c) *human intervention* repaired the hard cases. Premise 2 (single-agent approaches fail) is never tested: no single-agent baseline, no ablation, and the two cited "single-agent" systems are, per their own bibliography entries, an instruction-tuning paper and a self-collaboration framework. Premise 4 is true only in a weaker form: the generated solution beats the specialized method on exactly 8 of 16 tasks and trails it by up to 12.70 points, while the baselines receive no tuning budget comparable to the system's literature-reading planner, 10 repair attempts and, on 6 of 16 tasks (§6, Fig. 4: 62.5% full automation), human help. The largest win (Q16, +22.48 over FedWeIT; +16.15 over TARGET, Table 5) comes from a task flagged as needing the human-in-the-loop protocol, using client-side experience replay (a different resource setting from exemplar-free TARGET), and the algorithm is not described. Finally, the agent prompts hard-code the benchmark's dataset IDs and a UCI-HAR transform (App. A.4.2), so the system was engineered on the same 16 tasks it is evaluated on, with no held-out tasks. In short, the paper demonstrates that a scaffolded frontier LLM plus humans can produce competitive FL code for well-known tasks; it does not demonstrate that the *proposed system design* is what makes this possible. The authors must answer with a single-agent control, ablations and held-out tasks.

## EIC Meta-Review (Prof. Marguerite Oyelaran-Haas)

### The Case For and Against
- **For acceptance:** A timely, readable, well-documented system with full prompts and a benchmark of 16 FL tasks; the generated solutions beat FedAvg and FedProx on 15 of 16 tasks and show a very large gain on Q16; the authors are candid about cost and the limits of autonomy. A first paper on "agents that build FL systems" with a usable benchmark can be of interest to the ICLR agents audience.
- **Against acceptance:** The contribution's mechanism (three-phase multi-agent design) is never isolated: no ablation, no single-agent or other agent baseline, no variance, on a self-constructed benchmark that the system's prompts appear to be tailored to. Human intervention (6 of 16 tasks) is mixed into the headline tables without per-task disclosure, the specialized baselines are neither the strongest nor demonstrably tuned, and the claim "competitive with, and often superior to" hand-crafted methods corresponds to 8 wins and 8 losses against the specialized method.

## Editorial Decision Letter

Dear Authors,

Thank you for submitting "Helmsman: Autonomous Synthesis of Federated Learning Systems via Collaborative LLM Agents" to ICLR.

Automating the engineering of federated learning systems with LLM agents is a timely, ambitious topic, and releasing the full agent prompts and the 16-task query list is commendable.

**Overall Assessment:** The reviewers found the system clearly described and the problem relevant, and they appreciated the candid discussion of cost and the share of tasks that needed human help. They agreed, however, that the experiments do not yet establish that the proposed multi-agent design is responsible for the results, and that several claims are worded more strongly than the tables support.

**Key Strengths Identified by Reviewers:**
1. Clear, modular system description with full prompts and task configurations (R1-S1, R4-S1, R4-S3).
2. A relevant application and a benchmark of templated FL tasks across five sub-areas (R1-S2, R2-S1, R3-S3).
3. Honest reporting of autonomy limits and cost; informative continual-learning case study (R1-S3, R2-S3, R4-S4).

**Key Concerns Requiring Attention:**
1. No ablations and no single-agent or other agent baselines; the mechanism is untested (CONSENSUS-4: R1, R2, R4, DA).
2. Claims exceed the evidence: "competitive with, and often superior to" corresponds to 8 wins and 8 losses against the specialized method; "semantically correct" is certified by short smoke tests; "autonomous" and "deployment-ready" are not demonstrated (CONSENSUS-4).
3. Human interventions on 6 of 16 tasks are mixed into the headline tables; baselines are not shown to be tuned with comparable effort; no variance; unstated evaluation protocol; table inconsistencies (CONSENSUS-3).

The Devil's Advocate raised no CRITICAL issue.

**Decision: Borderline Reject (3)**

We think the work could become a solid contribution. What would change the decision: a single-agent and an ablation study at matched budgets, per-task intervention levels and fully automatic results, equal-effort strong baselines with variance, a clear evaluation protocol (no use of test-split feedback; held-out tasks), and corrected tables and claims. Please refer to the Revision Roadmap below.

Sincerely,
Prof. Marguerite Oyelaran-Haas
Editor-in-Chief (meta-reviewer)
