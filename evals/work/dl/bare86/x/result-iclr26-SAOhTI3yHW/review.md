# Review: RIFT: Group-Relative RL Fine-Tuning for Realistic and Controllable Traffic Simulation

(Based on extracted text. Figures were not available, so figure-dependent claims such as the Fig. 3 speed/acceleration distributions and the qualitative Figs. 4–5 are judged from captions and text only.)

## Summary
The paper proposes a two-stage, AV-centered closed-loop traffic simulation framework in CARLA/Bench2Drive. Stage one is an open-loop imitation-learned trajectory generator (the released Pluto checkpoint, trained on nuPlan). It controls a few "critical background vehicles" (CBVs), chosen by a route-level distance-to-goal heuristic. Stage two, RIFT, fine-tunes only Pluto's trajectory *scoring head* with closed-loop RL. All N_ref×N_lon candidate trajectories at a state are treated as a GRPO "group". Each gets a discounted sum of a hand-designed state-wise reward, and advantages are normalized within the group. The KL term is dropped and replaced by Dual-Clip PPO's lower bound on negative-advantage terms. On Bench2Drive dev10 routes, RIFT-Pluto is compared with RL, SFT, RLFT and hybrid baselines on infraction, progress, safety and "realism" metrics (Table 1). It is also used to generate traffic for evaluating two AV planners, PDM-Lite and PlanT (Table 2), and is ablated in Table 3.

## Strengths
- The problem is relevant: closed-loop traffic simulation that is both realistic and steerable. Freezing the pre-trained generator and fine-tuning only the scoring head is a sensible, cheap way to keep the learned trajectory distribution intact (about 8 GPU-hours per run).
- Using the full candidate set as a GRPO group fits a multimodal anchor/query-based decoder naturally. It gives a dense, critic-free learning signal per state.
- The baseline set is reasonably broad: PPO, REINFORCE and GRPO fine-tuning of the same module, SFT, and the RTR and RS hybrids. All fine-tuned baselines use the same module. Means and standard deviations over three seeds are reported.
- The aggressive-reward ablation shows that the reward steers behavior in the expected direction (more progress, more infractions). Limitations are stated honestly, including the state-wise reward and the restriction to vectorized-input AVs.

## Weaknesses
1. **"Realism" is not measured against real data.** The realism metrics are (i) the Shapiro–Wilk statistic of speed and acceleration, i.e. how "normal" the distribution looks, and (ii) the Wasserstein distance to a "target speed" in CARLA. A higher normality statistic is not evidence of human-like behavior. There is no comparison to logged-data distributions and no WOSAC-style likelihood metrics, despite the paper citing WOSAC. Nothing tests the central claim that multimodality is preserved, and diversity or coverage of generated behaviors is never measured. Since only the scoring head changes, the candidate set is identical to Pluto's by construction. What changes is which mode is selected, and RL can collapse that to a single mode. No entropy or mode-usage statistics are given. The realism metrics were also designed by the authors and are entangled with the reward.
2. **The claims are stronger than Table 1 supports.** The text says RIFT "consistently outperforms all baselines in both aspects". But the original Pluto has far lower ORR (0.24 vs 0.36) and CPK (5.06 vs 6.83) than RIFT. SFT-Pluto, PPO-Pluto and RTR-Pluto have lower or comparable infractions. RIFT's clear win is Route Progress (995 vs 893 for GRPO-Pluto), and progress is also what the authors' reward favors. Many safety differences (2D-TTC 2.74 vs 2.65, ACT 2.71 vs 2.61) lie within the reported standard deviations (±1.3–1.6). Acceleration SW is worse than GRPO-Pluto (0.93 vs 0.96), and speed WD is within noise of PPO-Pluto (4.46±0.43 vs 4.96±0.31). Dismissing the lower infractions of supervised methods as "conservatism" is plausible but untested. Infractions, progress and TTC are also not really controllability metrics.
3. **The dual-clip contribution is thin and its ablation cuts both ways.** Dual-clip is an existing technique from Dual-Clip PPO, and GRPO and dropping KL are also existing ideas, so the algorithmic novelty is mostly assembly. In the ablation (Table 3), the PPO-Clip variant has *better* ORR (0.04 vs 0.36) and CPK (2.03 vs 6.83) than RIFT. It has lower progress and speed realism, and the text reports only the latter. The claim that dual-clip "improves controllability and training stability" is backed by no training curves, no seed variance, and no evidence of instability. It is also unclear why the ratio would explode. The paper never defines π(τ|s) for a candidate (a softmax over the confidence scores?), and the policy takes only a few mini-batch steps from π_old. A study of c, ε, and the number of update epochs is needed. Removing KL is argued by assertion only ("unnecessary and may hinder alignment"), with no ablation that retains KL.
4. **The theory (Appendix A) is not meaningful.** It treats a binary reward and a one-dimensional "success-probability" map h_ε(p). It assumes a contraction with a Lipschitz constant L(c, ε) that is never computed and is made small by choosing a step size. The derivation of Φ(p)=2ε√(p(1−p)) assumes the update saturates at the clip boundaries for every p, and then treats gradient ascent on Φ as the dynamics. That is not a statement about the actual policy update. Fixing δ such that h(p0)∈[δ,1−δ] is circular. Existence of a fixed point says nothing about reaching an optimum or about stability under non-binary, discounted, state-wise rewards. Claim (c), "guaranteeing stable learning", does not follow from the bound on negative-advantage gradients, and Eq. (12) is not a meaningful statement about gradient magnitude. I would treat this as decoration rather than a contribution.
5. **The downstream "AV evaluation" argument is conceptually confused.** Section 5.3 and Table 2 claim RIFT is best because the AV obtains the highest driving score and lowest Blocked Rate (0.00 for PDM-Lite) in RIFT scenarios. A good evaluation environment for an AV is realistic *and* challenging. A simulator in which the AV scores 94.8 DS with zero blocking is partly an easier one (the CBVs yield or cooperate). The paper never distinguishes "realistic" from "benign". There is no evidence that the scenarios discriminate between AV planners, none that they surface realistic failures, and no correlation to real-world AV performance. For PlanT, RIFT is not the best (DS 44.3 vs 47.2 for GRPO-Pluto; IS 0.64 is the worst; BR 40 vs 33), which the text does not acknowledge, so "near-optimal DS, RC, IS" is an overstatement. The new BR metric is coarse: with 10 evaluation routes it moves in 10% steps, and it measures CBV behavior rather than scenario quality.
6. **Evaluation scale and generalization are limited.** Evaluation is on Bench2Drive dev10 (10 routes, 3 seeds), while training uses 2×Bench2Drive220 routes from the same CARLA towns and scenario families. There are no held-out towns, no other simulator or dataset (e.g., Waymax/WOSAC, nuPlan closed loop), no human reference, and no statistical tests. Table 2 differences are mostly indistinguishable given std up to 10–15 on BR. Only one backbone (Pluto), one reward family, one CBV per scene, and one planner of each type are tested.
7. **Framing issues.** The "dual-stage framework" is essentially fine-tuning a pre-trained policy in a new simulator, the standard practice in [21, 22, 43], yet it is presented as a main contribution. The distinction from prior work ("fine-tune only the best trajectory") is not clean, because the GRPO-Pluto baseline differs from RIFT only in the KL/dual-clip change. The "realism" comes from nuPlan, a different domain (maps, vehicle types) from CARLA. How nuPlan-style features are mapped to CARLA scenes, and the resulting domain gap, are not studied. The state-wise reward has no forward simulation (acknowledged), and the results depend strongly on its design. Beyond one "aggressive" variant there is no sensitivity analysis, and "controllability" is shown only by reweighting a reward, not by conditioning on user-specified goals. The claim of "user-aligned controllability" is therefore stronger than the evidence.
8. **Reproducibility and presentation.** Details that matter (the definition of π(τ|s), reward terms, c/ε/β values, the number of CBVs) are scattered or in the appendix. There are small notation inconsistencies: A is indexed to G while R is indexed to N_ref×N_lon, and Eq. (4) uses "SelfAttn(·, dim=0)" notation loosely. RP values above 100 are not defined in the main text.

## Questions
1. How is π_θ(τ_i|s) defined, given that the trajectories come from a frozen decoder and only the scorer changes? If it is a softmax over scores, what does "multimodality preserved" mean beyond mode selection? Please report score-distribution entropy or diversity of executed modes before and after fine-tuning.
2. Can you report realism against real logs, e.g. WOSAC-style likelihoods, distance-to-log distributions, or a human study?
3. Why does the PPO-Clip variant achieve much lower ORR/CPK? Do training curves show the claimed stability benefit of dual-clip? Please add "KL retained" ablations and sensitivity to c and ε.
4. What does the AV-evaluation experiment say about simulator *quality*? Can you show that RIFT scenarios discriminate between AV planners or expose realistic failures, e.g. with a safety-critical reward?
5. Is there any evaluation on towns or routes unseen in training? What is the train/test overlap?
6. How do results change with the number of CBVs, a different generator, or different reward functions?
7. How does Appendix A relate to the actual multi-step, non-binary, state-wise discounted reward training?

## Ratings
- **Soundness:** 2
- **Presentation:** 3
- **Contribution:** 2
- **Rating:** 4 (marginally below the acceptance threshold)
- **Confidence:** 4

The problem is practically relevant and the engineering is solid. But the realism evidence is weak, the headline claims exceed what Tables 1–3 show, the algorithmic novelty is modest (GRPO plus dual-clip on a scoring head), the theory is not meaningful, and the downstream evaluation conflates "easier" with "better".
