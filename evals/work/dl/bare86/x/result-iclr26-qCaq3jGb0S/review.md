# Review: Cache What Lasts: Token Retention for Memory-Bounded KV Cache in LLMs (TRIM-KV)

## Summary
The paper proposes TRIM-KV, a learned KV-cache eviction policy for pretrained LLMs. A small MLP gate per layer maps each token's hidden state to a per-KV-head retention score β∈[0,1]. The token's weight then decays as β^(t−i). During training the gate modulates attention logits (retention-gated attention, Eq. 3). Only the gates are trained, with a forward-KL distillation loss, a next-token loss, and a hinge capacity loss on Σ β^(t−i) (Eq. 5). At inference the gate is used only to rank tokens: when the cache exceeds M, the token with the smallest β_j^(t−j) is evicted. Experiments cover Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1-Distill models on AIME24, GSM8K and MATH-500, LongProc, LongMemEval_S and SCBench. The method beats SnapKV, H2O, StreamingLLM and R-KV, and often beats SeerAttn-R. At small budgets it sometimes exceeds the full cache. The paper also offers qualitative analyses of the learned scores (sinks, sliding windows, gist-like period tokens) and a throughput table.

## Strengths
- The idea is simple, well motivated and cheap. Importance is predicted at creation time, so no attention statistics are needed at eviction time. Eviction costs O(M) and is compatible with FlashAttention-style kernels. The design is practical, and plausibly faster than R-KV or SnapKV (Table 5).
- Training is lightweight: only the gates are trained, on frozen backbones, on 4 H100s. A FlexAttention and Triton implementation supports sequences up to 128K.
- The decay formulation β^(t−i) is a neat differentiable relaxation of the binary eviction variable. It keeps train and inference close, and it recovers standard attention when β=1.
- The empirical gains are large in the low-budget regime on reasoning benchmarks, across several model sizes and families. TRIM-KV beats R-KV and SnapKV even when they get 4× the budget. Beyond math, it generalises to LongProc, even though the gates were trained on math data. On LongMemEval it keeps near-full accuracy at 25% of the budget (48.2 vs 49.4).
- The ablation of the capacity loss is informative (AIME 74.0 → 42.9 without it). The qualitative head and layer analyses are interesting and could be useful for budget allocation.

## Weaknesses
1. **Baseline fairness.** The gates are trained on OpenR1-Math (564M tokens) and evaluated on math reasoning, so TRIM-KV is trained in-domain while the heuristic baselines are training-free. That is a legitimate difference, but the headline claims ("198% relative improvement", "58.4% gain") are computed at specific budgets that favour the method. The 58.4% appears to be an average or a selected setting, and the paper does not say which. The comparison to SeerAttn-R should be at matched training data and compute, and the paper should say whether SeerAttn-R was retrained, run from released checkpoints, or run with different budget semantics. SeerAttn-R is block-sparse over a full cache, so "same budget" is not obviously the same quantity.
2. **Missing learned-eviction baselines.** The introduction argues that existing learned eviction methods (Locret, NACL and similar) are limited to prefill, but none is compared. A simple trained baseline would help isolate what is new: Locret-style retaining heads fed into the same eviction loop, or a gate trained without the decay and capacity terms. In the SCBench table, SnapKV and H2O are run in a prefill-compression setting, which may not be their intended use case.
3. **Statistical rigor.** The AIME24 numbers use 64 samples, but there are no confidence intervals or standard errors. Several "surpasses full cache" claims (e.g. Qwen3-4B AIME24, 74.0 at a 4096 budget vs 65.5 for the full cache) rest on single runs. A 65.5 full-KV score for Qwen3-4B is low relative to commonly reported figures. This suggests a particular evaluation setup, such as a generation length cap. If the full-cache baseline is truncated by a max-length limit, or eviction truncates degenerate loops, the "regularisation" reading is not supported. The regularisation explanation is speculative and untested. Alternatives include shorter reasoning traces, avoiding repetition loops, and the gates having been trained on the same data distribution. The paper should report generation lengths and test these alternatives.
4. **Mismatch between training and inference.** Training uses a soft multiplicative modulation of attention logits, exp(β^(t−i) q·k). Inference uses hard eviction with unmodified attention scores. The retained tokens at inference are attended with full weight, which is a different function from the one trained. The paper says that it "bridges the gap" but gives no analysis of the discrepancy. Note also that the logit modulation scales q·k rather than adding a log-decay bias, so an old token with a negative q·k is up-weighted, not down-weighted. Why this parameterisation, rather than an additive log β^(t−i) bias, is not justified. The capacity loss uses M=512 as a "soft" hyperparameter, yet inference budgets range from 128 to 32K. The sensitivity to this mismatch is explored only in the appendix.
5. **Claims on "intrinsic importance".** The retention score depends only on the token's own hidden state at creation time. This cannot capture future-query dependence, and the paper does not test where it fails. SCBench shows complete failure on Retr.KV (0.0) and Code.RepoQA (4.3). The authors attribute this to incompressible contexts, but a query-aware method would be a sensible comparison. TRIM-KV also loses on Math.Find to some baselines. Retr.MultiHop is strong (43.1), which deserves analysis. The abstract says it "consistently outperforms" baselines, which is stronger than the SCBench table supports. The abstract also lists LongBenchV2, but the main text only references LongBench results in the appendix.
6. **Interpretability claims are overreaching.** The "new path toward interpretability", "newly observed eviction patterns" and the "periods as gist tokens" hypothesis rest on hand-picked heads from one AIME example, with no quantitative or causal validation (e.g., ablating those heads or tokens). Retention scores are also a product of the training objective and the specific capacity loss, so they need not reflect a head's function in the base model. Statements like "human intuition" are subjective.
7. **Efficiency evidence is thin and the budget is not truly per-head.** The throughput table is for a single setup (H200, M=1024). The paper does not report memory, per-head variable budgets (left to future work), or the overhead of the gate at short context. The cache budget is uniform across heads, so the claimed benefits of heterogeneous budget allocation are not realised.
8. **Generalisation limits.** Gates are trained per model and per data domain. The cost of retraining is acknowledged only implicitly. Results use models up to 14B and contexts of about 128K. Interaction with quantisation or multi-turn prefix caching is not discussed. The claim of a "globally optimal caching policy" (Sec. 4.2) is unsupported; joint training does not guarantee global optimality.
9. **Presentation.** The text has typos ("Patero frontiers"), and the main text leans on the appendix for essential results (LongProc full table, ablations, throughput). The pass@1 gain percentages are hard to verify from the figures. I could not inspect the figures, so figure-dependent claims are judged only from captions and text.

## Questions
1. Which exact budgets and settings produce the "198%" and "58.4%" numbers? Please give per-model, per-budget tables with confidence intervals or seeds.
2. How were SeerAttn-R and R-KV run (retrained, released checkpoints, same generation cap)? Why is the Qwen3-4B full-KV AIME24 score 65.5? Please report average generation lengths and the fraction of truncated or looping outputs for full KV versus TRIM-KV.
3. Can you compare against a learned decode-time eviction baseline (e.g., Locret-style heads, or a gate trained with only distillation and a top-k mask), and against TRIM-KV gates trained without the exponential decay (a fixed score)?
4. Does the hard-eviction inference model deviate from the soft training model? Please measure the KL or accuracy gap between retention-gated attention (soft) and actual eviction at matched budget. Why modulate the logits as β^(t−i)·q·k instead of adding (t−i)·log β?
5. How sensitive is the method to M used in training when the inference budget is far larger or smaller (e.g., train M=512, test 32K)? How does the method do if the gates are trained on non-math data and tested on math?
6. Is there any quantitative evidence, such as head ablations, supporting the interpretability and gist-token claims?
7. Why does TRIM-KV score 0 on SCBench Retr.KV while the full cache scores 66? Would a hybrid with a few attention-guided tokens help?

## Scores
- Soundness: 3
- Presentation: 3
- Contribution: 3
- **Rating: 6** (marginally above the acceptance threshold)
- **Confidence: 4**

The method is simple, practical and shows strong results on reasoning workloads. The main reservations are the in-domain training advantage over training-free baselines, the lack of statistical reporting and of learned-eviction baselines, the unexplained "beats full cache" results, and the overreaching interpretability claims. I lean towards acceptance.
