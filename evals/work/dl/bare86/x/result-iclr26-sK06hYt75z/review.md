# Review: Localized Cultural Knowledge is Conserved and Controllable in LLMs

(Based on the main text, lines 1–645 of the extracted file. I did not read the appendices. Figures were unavailable, so I judged them from captions and text only.)

## Summary
The paper studies the gap between "explicit" localization (a prompt prefix such as "I live in X") and "implicit" localization (only the prompt language signals the culture). It calls this the explicit–implicit localization gap. It builds a small two-option benchmark in five languages: names, cities, o1-distilled questions and CulturalBench. Seven models are evaluated.

Main findings:
1. The gap is typically above 10 points and as large as 68 points.
2. A single culturally relevant word (a dish, currency or city) closes most of the gap.
3. Explicit prompting makes open-ended generations more homogeneous and more stereotypical.
4. Activation patching on Gemma-2-9b-it shows that localization is written in at mid layers (about 23–30).
5. A contrastive-activation-addition steering vector, "I live in X" minus control, recovers part of the explicit gain, mostly preserves diversity and is judged less stereotypical.
6. The vector transfers across tasks and across languages, and a leave-one-out "universal" vector gives a partial improvement.

## Strengths
- The explicit vs. implicit framing is clear and relevant. It makes a real point about how benchmark methodology differs from how users actually prompt.
- The 2×2 design is simple and controls for answer-option order by averaging both orderings.
- Combining behavioural evaluation, patching and steering gives a coherent story. Cross-task and cross-language transfer of the vector is an interesting result.
- The paper reports trade-offs of explicit prompting (homogeneity, stereotypes) rather than only its accuracy gains. It is also open about limitations.

## Weaknesses
1. **Benchmark validity and construction.** The core benchmark is binary, and one option is always American. A model (or a steering vector) can score well by learning "pick the non-American answer", or by default to "non-English-looking". The authors acknowledge this for the steering vector and only add a multi-culture check in the appendix. Names and cities are tiny, synthetic and low-ambiguity. The o1-distilled questions are unvalidated by native speakers. Translation quality is unverified. "Localized" is defined as the country-specific answer, so intra-country variation is ignored. Overall, the headline gap is measured on very easy, narrow tasks, and its size (up to 68%) is not evidence of general cultural competence.
2. **The gap metric is confounded.** The EI-Gap compares English + context against translated, no-context prompts, so it conflates language effects (translation and lower-resource performance) with the effect of context. In Figure 2 the "United States" row also shows a large gap (English + USA context vs. English), which the authors admit is unexplained. A cleaner decomposition would compare translated + context vs. translated, and English + context vs. English. The definition in Sec. 2.4 uses probabilities, but the results are reported as accuracy differences. The "gap" is also reported with no uncertainty. The statement "the gap in Gemma-27b is 22% vs 35% in 9b" is not tested for significance.
3. **Explicit prompting is a weak baseline, and the conclusion is partly artefactual.** The explicit condition is the fixed string "I live in [country]". The paper shows a single cultural word closes the gap, which suggests the gap is largely prompt-format sensitivity, not missing knowledge. Claims that knowledge is "conserved" and "controllable" rest on the same observation that any cue elicits it. No comparison is made with a system-prompt persona, few-shot examples or fine-tuning. Steering also falls short of explicit prompting on accuracy (Table 2: e.g. 0.67–0.80 vs. 1.0 on cities, 0.61–0.64 vs. 0.79 on o1-distilled; Table 4: 0.64–0.78 vs. 0.86–0.91). So "dramatically improve the potential for customization" is overstated.
4. **Diversity and stereotype claims are weakly supported.** Homogeneity is measured as the cosine similarity of OpenAI text-embedding-3-small embeddings. The differences are tiny (e.g. 0.337 → 0.368; GPT-4o 0.317 → 0.324, within error bars) and the metric is not validated as a measure of diversity. The comparison with steering is on one model (Gemma-2-9b), and for English steering it is not clearly lower. Table 3 shows the steered homogeneity is closer to implicit in some languages but not by a significant margin. Embedding similarity also depends on topic and language. There are only 24 stories, and the stereotypicality and faithfulness results are LLM-judge arena ratings with no human validation or judge-bias checks. Whether less stereotypical output reflects a better localized output or merely less localization (less culture content) is not separated: the steered model localizes less, so it would naturally produce fewer stereotypes. A user study is acknowledged as missing.
5. **Mechanistic analysis is thin and single-model.** Patching and steering are done only on Gemma-2-9b-it. The claim of "universal" mechanisms, and the "consolidation of a world model", is not backed by multi-model evidence. Patching is done at the last token with changed option labels; the interpretation of the probability curves (late layers return the source answer because "the attention is unable to write") is speculative and cannot be checked without the figure. The steering vector is computed as a difference of means between *different prompts* (a departure from CAA), and which hyperparameters (layer, α) were selected and on which data is relegated to the appendix. If these were tuned on the evaluation set, the reported rates are optimistic. No random-vector or non-cultural-contrast controls are reported. Because the vector is derived from "I live in X" minus control, it may encode "a country mention", "the first-person context" or "not-USA", not "culture".
6. **Overclaiming on universality.** The "universal" vector is based on four non-English cultures, evaluated on a leave-one-out basis, gives only a modest gain over implicit (e.g. France 0.597 → 0.638; Turkey 0.560 → 0.678), and is, by the authors' own admission, not rigorously evaluated. The abstract nonetheless says that a vector is "conserved across all non-English languages", and the introduction says "mechanisms are culture-agnostic". The note that the vector may only mean "non-American" applies here too. Table 4 gives the US row a steered result of 0.74 even though the US is the default culture, which hints at the vector acting more as a "pick the other option" bias.
7. **Scope and generality.** Five languages (and country = language, ignoring e.g. multi-country languages such as French and English), a handful of mid-sized open models (and GPT-4o as a black box, with a 64% refusal rate on city questions that complicates its numbers), and no reasoning models or newer frontier models. The claim "LLMs default to English-centric responses" is demonstrated only on forced-choice tasks. Steering requires white-box access, which excludes the most widely used chatbots the paper motivates itself with. Whether steering still works on longer, real conversations is unclear.
8. **Presentation.** The text is mostly readable, but the key details (steering prompts, layer/α selection, dataset sizes, judge prompts) are deferred to the appendix. Figures 2 and 3 are dense heatmaps. The EI-Gap definition does not match how it is reported. There are sloppy statements: "the gap in performance is usually above 10%" without saying whether this is relative or absolute, and "homogeneity goes up (except for Aya, which was not evaluated)".

## Questions
- How large are the datasets, and how many items per language? Were translations checked by native speakers? What are the confidence intervals for Fig. 2/3?
- Is the EI-Gap computed against English implicit, or translated implicit? Can you report the decomposition (language effect vs. context effect)?
- Were the layer and α hyperparameters chosen on held-out data? Do random-direction vectors or a "mention a random country" vector produce similar effects? Does a vector from an unrelated contrast ("I live in X" for X in the US) produce a "non-American" bias?
- Does steering degrade general capabilities, fluency, or answers to culture-neutral questions? Does it produce correct answers in the multi-culture multi-choice task, where the "non-American" shortcut is unavailable (main text results rather than the appendix)?
- Do the patching and steering findings hold for other models (Llama, Qwen, larger Gemma)?
- Can you validate the LLM-judge stereotype ranking with humans, and show that steered outputs aren't simply less culturally specific?

## Ratings
- **Soundness: 2** (the main claims are plausible, but the benchmark is a binary and confounded design, the controls are missing, and the diversity/stereotype evidence is weak.)
- **Presentation: 3**
- **Contribution: 2** (a useful framing and a sensible application of known tools, but the finding is incremental and limited in scope.)
- **Rating: 4** (marginally below the acceptance threshold)
- **Confidence: 4**
