# Review: Splat Regression Models (ICLR 2026)

(Based on extracted text only; figures not visible, judged from captions and text. Appendices were not in the extracted text, so the proofs are unchecked.)

## Summary
The paper proposes Splat Regression Models (SRMs): f(x) = Σ_i v_i ρ_{A_i,b_i}(x), a mixture of anisotropic bump functions (affine pushforwards of a "mother splat" ρ) with vector weights. It is a two-layer network with a Gaussian-like activation, equivalently an RBF network with learned full covariances. The authors place parameters on a "Bures-Wasserstein" manifold and view the model as a measure over (v, splat). They derive Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao gradients of F(f_µ) (Thm 1), state universal approximation (a corollary of Cybenko plus a Lipschitz bound with k ≲ ε^{-2(d+2)}), and claim that 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a special case, with its heuristics (pruning, noise injection) interpretable as WFR flow components. Experiments cover (i) a 1D multiscale fit versus Chebyshev and Haar wavelets, (ii) 2D noisy regression versus MLP and KAN, and (iii) a 2D Allen-Cahn PINN problem versus MLP and KAN. SRMs are reported to beat MLP and KAN by about an order of magnitude with far fewer parameters.

## Strengths
- Clear, simple model with a clean abstraction (conditional mean under a measure over (v, splat)). The unified view of splatting as "model / inverse problem / optimizer" is a useful framing for a theory-minded audience.
- The geometry (Bures-Wasserstein structure of affine pushforwards; WFR gradients in splat coordinates) is mathematically neat. A principled Fisher-Rao (birth-death) pruning criterion is an appealing idea if it can be shown to work.
- The experiments are easy to follow and the 1D "adaptive grid" intuition (Fig. 1) is nice. The paper is candid that overfitting is a limitation.
- Low-dimensional regression and PINN problems are a sensible target for local-basis models.

## Weaknesses
1. **Novelty relative to RBF / Gaussian-mixture networks.** With ρ = N(0, I), the model is exactly an anisotropic RBF network, or a Gaussian-mixture regression with learned means and covariances. Such models (adaptive RBF networks, kernel-mixture regression, Gaussian mixture layers [CRY25], adaptive finite elements / free-knot splines) have a long history. The related-work discussion does not engage with RBF networks, free-knot splines, or adaptive-grid methods. KAN-as-RBF [Li24] is cited, but the closest classical baselines are not compared. The universal approximation result is an immediate corollary of Cybenko, so it adds little.
2. **The theoretical contribution is thin and partly misdescribed.** In the (v, A, b) coordinates, the "Wasserstein gradient" of Thm 1 is, up to a mass factor, just the ordinary Euclidean gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters, i.e. standard backpropagation. The formulas follow by the chain rule. The experiments use Adam (Sec. 4.2) or plain gradient descent (Sec. 4.1), not an actual WFR flow. Nothing in the experiments uses the Fisher-Rao component (no birth/death, no reweighting). So "principled optimization via WFR gradient flows, which are crucial to our empirical success" is not supported. No convergence result is given. The Chizat-Bach style conditional global convergence is mentioned in related work but never established for this geometry. Several displayed formulas also look questionable (e.g., the Poisson-example gradients in Example 2 place ∆ρ where the chain rule would give the adjoint operator applied to the residual; the Fisher-Rao gradient is not tied to a defined metric normalization). I could not check the proofs in the appendix.
3. **The claim of "recovering Gaussian Splatting as a special case" is not substantiated.** Example 2 only writes down the volume-rendering formulation. The actual 3DGS pipeline (projection of 3D Gaussians to 2D, depth sorting, α-compositing, spherical harmonics, densification/clone/split, opacity resets) is not shown to be an instance of the framework or of WFR descent. The statements that the heuristics "can be interpreted" as regularized risk minimization, that noise injection is an entropic regularizer, and that birth-death gives a pruning criterion "guaranteed to decrease the loss" are speculation without derivation or experiments. There is no novel-view-synthesis experiment at all, even though NVS is the stated motivation. Also, 3DGS opacity/occlusion (non-linear transmittance) is not a mixture of the form f_µ, so the unification applies at best to the emission field.
4. **Experiments are small and the baselines weak or possibly under-tuned.** All experiments are 1D/2D toy problems with one smooth function or one PDE. There are no seeds, no error bars, no repeat runs. In Sec. 4.2, MLPs and KANs are trained with Adam at lr 1e-4 for 10^4 steps on 1000 points with a single config. The MLPs use ReLU (poorly suited to smooth targets and PDE residuals; no Fourier features, SIREN or tanh), and the KANs use a fixed grid of 5 with order-10 splines (an odd choice). There is no per-model tuning and no stronger low-dimensional baselines (RBF networks with trainable centers/widths, GP regression, SIREN, Fourier-feature MLPs, hash-grid/Instant-NGP, adaptive FEM, spectral methods). The paper itself says "smart positional encoding is all you need," so it should compare with positional-encoding MLPs. The Allen-Cahn setting with ε = 0.1 is mild, and no comparison with a standard numerical solver is given. Parameter-count comparisons are not like-for-like, and the test function sin(3π√x)cos(3πy) is separable, which favours axis-aligned local bumps. The PINN error metric is not clearly defined. Figure 1 shows the splat model slightly worse than Chebyshev at equal parameters, so "competes with the gold standard" is modest. The sawtooth case is relegated to the appendix.
5. **Scalability and dimension.** The quantitative bound k ≲ ε^{-2(d+2)} is the curse of dimensionality, and the paper restricts itself to low d without evidence at d ≥ 3 (no 3D PDE, no real data). Cost per step (n·k evaluations, a dense d×d matrix per splat, inverting A_i) is not discussed, nor is the stability of A_i (singular matrices, the det A^{-1} factor). Overfitting is acknowledged but not studied, and no regularization is proposed. There is no ablation on mother splat choice, k, initialization, or optimizer (WFR vs Adam vs plain SGD), though the paper's own claims concern these.
6. **Presentation.** There are many typos and rough spots (garbled text in Prop. 2, "my minimizing", duplicated references KKLD23a/b, MST+21a/b, MMM19a/b). "Theorem 3 (Informal)" is not rigorous. Contribution (4), an "instruction manual for deploying and training," is not delivered: there are no practical recommendations or ablations. There is over-claiming throughout ("highly expressive," "vastly outperforming").

## Questions
1. How does an SRM with Gaussian ρ differ from an RBF network with trainable centers and full covariance, or from Gaussian-mixture regression? Please compare to these baselines directly.
2. Do any experiments use the Fisher-Rao component or the WFR flow? What does the Wasserstein/WFR step provide relative to plain Adam on (v, A, b)? Please include an ablation (Adam vs. WGD vs. WFR with birth-death).
3. Can you give a precise statement, with proof, that the 3DGS objective and optimizer are an instance of your framework? How does occlusion/transmittance fit in? Can you provide an NVS experiment, even on a small synthetic scene?
4. Can you show results with multiple seeds, tuned baselines (SIREN, Fourier-feature MLPs, tanh PINNs, hash grids, RBF), harder PDEs (smaller ε, higher dimension, time-dependent), and real data?
5. How do cost and accuracy scale with d (d = 3, 4, 5)? What happens with ill-conditioned A_i?
6. What is the normalization of the Fisher-Rao gradient, and does it correspond to a valid metric on S_{p,d}? Are the Example 2 gradients correct as written?
