# Review: Dataset Distillers Are Good Label Denoisers In the Wild

## Summary
The paper proposes using dataset distillation (DD) as an offline label-denoising step for learning from noisy labels. It benchmarks three existing DD methods (DATM, DANCE, RCIG) on CIFAR-10/100 and Tiny-ImageNet with symmetric noise, on CIFAR-10/100 with asymmetric noise, and on CIFAR-10N/100N with human noise. It reports three observations. (I) With symmetric noise, a distilled set can beat training on the full noisy set when noise is high, even at very small IPC. (II) Asymmetric noise is absorbed into the distilled data and DD does not help. (III) With natural noise, DD helps at high noise (CIFAR-N "Worst") but not at low noise ("Aggre"). Two "corollaries" give an IPC upper bound (Eq. 10) and a noise-rate lower bound (Eq. 11). The paper also claims that DD avoids "vicious cycles", improves efficiency and gives privacy protection, and that tail-class samples suffer lossy compression.

## Strengths
- The question is simple and interesting: can DD's tendency to capture common patterns be repurposed against memorization of noisy labels?
- The study spans three DD families, three noise types and several datasets. The negative findings (asymmetric noise, low-noise regimes) are reported honestly.
- The asymmetric-noise explanation (structured noise is "common pattern" and is therefore distilled) is intuitive and plausible.

## Weaknesses
1. **No comparison with noisy-label learning baselines.** The paper never compares against standard LNL methods (DivideMix, co-teaching, GCE, ELR, DISC, etc.), nor against simple baselines such as early stopping or small-loss selection followed by random subsampling. The comparison is "distilled set of IPC=k vs. full noisy training with plain CE", so the claim that DD is a "good label denoiser" is unsupported. The full-data baseline is itself weak: a ConvNet with plain CE at 60–80% symmetric noise. A random or coreset subset of equal size, which is the obvious control, is missing. Smaller training sets, early stopping and strong regularization can all improve over memorizing noisy labels. The gains may reflect a generic benefit of low-capacity or low-data training rather than denoising by distillation.
2. **The central premise (avoiding the "vicious cycle") is argued rather than shown.** The distillers (e.g., DATM's expert trajectories, RCIG's inner loop) are themselves trained on the noisy data, so they also depend on noisy-model quality. Nothing demonstrates that this is any less circular than noise-evaluation pipelines. At low noise the methods are worse than the baseline, and for asymmetric noise they fail. These are exactly the regimes where noisy-label methods are most relevant and where a practitioner cannot know the noise type in advance.
3. **Practical applicability is unclear.** Distillation of the noisy data is far more expensive than standard LNL training. Expert-trajectory training for DATM, bi-level optimization for RCIG and large IPC all cost far more. The "improves training efficiency" claim is not measured (no wall-clock or compute numbers). Evaluation is restricted to tiny ConvNets and small images; the scalability issues the paper itself mentions (ImageNet) mean the "in the wild" title is not supported. Clothing1M and WebVision are mentioned in Sec. 3.1 but never used. The best results at high noise are still low in absolute terms, since small-IPC distilled sets have low accuracy even on clean data.
4. **Privacy claim is unsupported.** "Strong privacy protection through offline processing" is asserted in the abstract and the contributions, with no threat model, no membership-inference or reconstruction analysis, and no differential-privacy guarantee. DD outputs are known to leak information and to be partially invertible. Offline processing alone is not privacy protection.
5. **The corollaries are not rigorous.** Corollary I (Eq. 10) bounds IPC by |S̃|(1−τ)/C, which just counts clean samples per class. This is trivial and does not follow from any property of distillation, and "perfectly preserve" is undefined. Corollary II (Eq. 11) claims a probability bound with δ, but no distributional assumption, proof or definition of the probability space is given. It is not clear what is random, and it cannot be right as stated: "accuracy matches the original dataset" does not imply τ ≥ 1 − C·IPC/|S̃|. Neither corollary is validated empirically. Using distillation results to estimate the noise rate is suggested and never tested.
6. **Evidence for the claims is thin and visual.** Results are presented only as curves (figures not inspectable by me, so I judge from the text). There are no tables, no variance or seeds, and no statistical significance. Observation-I says a single image per class surpasses the baseline at τ=0.6/0.8, but the baseline numbers are not stated in the text. Hyperparameters (distillation iterations, evaluation architectures, number of runs, whether labels are learned in DATM/RCIG, soft-label use) are not given. Soft labels and learned labels in these DD methods can themselves change the labels, which matters for "label denoising". The tail-class/imbalance claim is only hypothesized ("we further hypothesize") and is not tested, yet it appears in the abstract and contributions.
7. **Little analysis of why.** There is no mechanism study (e.g., what fraction of noisy samples influence the synthetic set, label-flip statistics of the distilled data, or whether distilled images reflect clean-class features). The claim that DD "captures common patterns while ignoring outliers" is untested. Figure 3 is qualitative only. The cross-method differences (DATM being best on asymmetric noise) are not explained.
8. **Presentation and framing.** The paper is largely an exploratory benchmark with no new method, yet the abstract and conclusion frame it as "a novel approach" with strong promise. Observations and "insights" overstate what is shown, for instance "rigorously evaluate" with 3 methods and small datasets. There are typos ("Realted Works", "bellow"). The related-work discussion of DD is long, while discussion of the coreset/subset-selection-under-noise literature and of DD robustness work is missing.

## Questions
1. How does a distilled set compare with equal-size random subsets, small-loss coresets, early-stopped training, and standard LNL methods (e.g., DivideMix, ELR) at the same noise rates?
2. Do the gains persist with a stronger full-data baseline (a ResNet, augmentation, early stopping)? Does DD on noisy data approach DD on clean data, and how much of the clean-data DD accuracy is recovered?
3. What are the measured compute/time costs relative to LNL pipelines supporting the "efficiency" claim?
4. What privacy guarantee do you claim, and against what adversary? Have you tested membership inference or inversion on the distilled sets?
5. What is the formal statement and proof of Corollary II? What is random in the probability, and how do you obtain δ?
6. Do the findings hold across seeds, with what variance? Do they hold on larger or real-world datasets (Clothing1M, WebVision, ImageNet subsets) and on non-ConvNet architectures?
7. Can distilled labels (soft or learned) be inspected to show that noisy labels are actually removed?

## Ratings
- Soundness: 2
- Presentation: 2
- Contribution: 2
- **Rating: 2**
- **Confidence: 4**

The study asks an interesting question, but the missing baselines, unsupported privacy and efficiency claims, non-rigorous corollaries and limited experiments (small scale, no variance) do not support the paper's headline claims. The results are mixed, with failure on asymmetric and low-noise regimes. In its current form it is below the bar for ICLR.
