# Review: HUME: Measuring the Human-Model Performance Gap in Text Embedding Tasks

(Text-only review. Figures were not available, so I judged them from captions and text. I read the main text and the first part of the appendix, up to about the task-example figures.)

## Summary
The paper introduces HUME, a protocol for collecting human performance on MTEB-style embedding tasks. Annotators worked in Argilla on 16 datasets across reranking, classification, clustering and STS, with languages including English, Arabic, Russian, Danish and Norwegian. Each dataset was downsampled to 20–50 instances (30 items for clustering). English tasks had two annotators and multilingual tasks one. Human scores are computed with MTEB metrics and compared with 13 embedding models on the same instances. The headline result is that humans average 77.6, which ranks 4th behind the best models (best model 80.1). Humans beat all models on non-English sentiment and Arabic STS22, and lose on clustering and reranking. The authors argue that low human agreement (Emotion κ=0.39, ArXiv clustering ARI≈0) signals flawed datasets. They also argue that "superhuman" model performance on such datasets reflects pattern matching on arbitrary labels. They recommend replacing some MTEB datasets and reporting model scores alongside human agreement.

## Strengths
- The question is useful. MTEB scores have no human reference, and a human baseline is a natural addition for interpreting them. The work could help benchmark curators.
- Humans and models are scored on identical instances with the same metrics, which is the right design for a direct comparison.
- The task coverage is reasonably broad (four task types, five languages, mixed natural and synthetic datasets). Reporting inter-annotator agreement alongside performance is good practice.
- The data, code and leaderboard are said to be released, and the subsets are included in the MTEB package.
- Some observations are interesting and plausible. Examples are the near-zero ARI on ArXiv topic clustering, the fair κ on Emotion, and the STS22-Russian parsing artifacts. The paper also acknowledges that human performance is not a ceiling.

## Weaknesses
1. **Statistical power is very low, and no uncertainty is reported.** Each dataset has 20–50 instances, and clustering uses only 30 items. A single accuracy on 40 items has a standard error of about 7 points. The headline "77.6 vs 80.1" and the ranking ("4th of 14") are therefore not interpretable without bootstrap CIs. Neither the human-vs-model margins nor the model-model ordering is tested. The win rates in Fig. 3 (e.g., "67% vs best", "29% vs 0%") are computed over 3–7 tasks per cell and are essentially anecdotal. Clustering with 30 items and V-measure is particularly unstable, and the paper does not say whether the number of clusters was given to annotators.
2. **Human performance is mostly a single annotator, so "human performance" is not well defined.** Multilingual tasks (all Arabic, Russian, Danish and Norwegian results) have one annotator. The paper's main "humans beat models" claims (Arabic sentiment 95.0, Russian 92.5, Norwegian 85.0, Arabic STS22 67.5) rest on one person on about 40 items each. There is no agreement estimate for these tasks, no repeat measurement, and no separation of annotator idiosyncrasy from a language effect. The limitations section says three annotators took part overall. All are male, aged 20–35 and NLP practitioners. The claim of a "cultural" advantage cannot be separated from annotator identity.
3. **The central causal narrative is unsupported.** The paper claims that models are "superhuman" where humans disagree because models "reproduce arbitrary label patterns". No analysis tests this: there are no error analyses, no label-noise estimates, and no comparison of model predictions against human-consensus labels. Section 5.1 itself concedes that "the mechanisms remain unclear", yet the abstract and conclusion state the conclusion as fact. A more mundane alternative is never considered. Many embedding models are trained or tuned on these datasets or similar ones, so an in-distribution advantage (and possible contamination) could explain the model-human gap. Humans see about 40 items with a guideline and no training.
4. **The task protocol is not comparable between humans and models.**
   - Classification: MTEB scores models with a logistic-regression probe trained on the training set. Humans receive only guidelines and so face the label-definition problem without supervision. A 45.8% human score on six-class Emotion reflects ambiguity of label semantics at least as much as dataset quality. The probe learns dataset-specific labelling conventions that humans cannot learn from 40 items.
   - Clustering: humans freely assign cluster IDs, while models use k-means with a known k. The paper does not say how humans were instructed about the number or granularity of clusters. An ARI of −0.001 on ArXiv could result from the instruction design and 30 items, rather than from "complete breakdown of human agreement" about the dataset.
   - Reranking: binary relevance judgments are scored as MAP. The paper does not describe how a ranking is derived from binary labels (ties, candidate-set size). Agreement is reported as mean Spearman/Kendall tau on these binary judgments, which is an odd statistic. Candidate pool sizes are not stated in the main text.
   - STS: humans use a 0–5 integer scale, and Spearman against gold on 30–50 pairs is compared with models' cosine similarity. These are different measurement instruments.
   The paper presents the comparison as "aligned", but the alignment is only nominal.
5. **Reading the results as a data-quality diagnosis is overreaching and partly circular.** Low human agreement is taken as evidence of a bad dataset. Yet agreement is estimated from two annotators and 30–48 items, and the thresholds are applied loosely (κ=0.55 with 77.8% consensus is called "reliable" for Toxicity, while κ=0.48 on Tweet sentiment is "reasonable"). The recommendation to replace Emotion and ArXiv clustering in MTEB is not justified. Emotion's label noise is well known, but the paper gives no evidence beyond a small kappa. It also does not check whether the ArXiv failure comes from the label hierarchy or from the sampling. The "inverse relationship" between human agreement and model performance is asserted from a handful of points with no correlation statistic or CI. The recommendations are also in tension: "prioritize high-agreement tasks" sits uneasily with STS22, which is both the source of the largest human advantage (Arabic) and a dataset the authors flag for parsing artifacts (Russian).
6. **Aggregation and reporting are questionable.** The "overall" score averages 26 task-language pairs of heterogeneous metrics (MAP, accuracy, V-measure, Spearman), with English weighted heavily (four datasets per category) and single-dataset languages weighted equally. The 77.6 human score is only 0.1 above gte-Qwen2-1.5B (77.5), so the "4th place" ranking is well within noise. There are inconsistencies: the Table 2 caption says humans are top in "5 of 14 aggregated pairs", the text uses 26 pairs, and elsewhere "3 of 7 classification tasks". "67% win rate against the best models" for Arabic is not clearly defined. "185% of human performance" (85/45.8) is not a meaningful statistic for a six-class task.
7. **The "framework" contribution is thin.** The contribution is essentially a small annotation study with Argilla interfaces. The claim of a "generalizable framework" is not backed by a specified protocol: guidelines are shown only as screenshots, and the recruitment, payment, time, training and quality controls are not described. A reader cannot reproduce the protocol from the main text. Related work is brief. It does not discuss prior approaches to estimating label noise from disagreement or to human ceilings and human label variation beyond a single citation.
8. **Conclusions about low-resource languages are not supported.** The abstract says the study reveals shortcomings in low-resource languages. Only Danish and Norwegian are low/mid-resource, and models do well on Danish and Norwegian reranking and sentiment. Several models in the pool (all-MiniLM, all-mpnet, mxbai, Jasper-en, stella-en) are English-only, so their poor non-English scores say nothing about multilingual weaknesses. The Arabic gaps are driven in part by this. The multilingual comparison is never isolated (e.g., to multilingual-e5-large and Qwen3 only). The pool also omits the strongest recent multilingual embedding models, so "best embedding model" is not necessarily state of the art.

## Questions
1. Can you provide bootstrap CIs (over items) for each human score, for the 77.6 overall, and for the human-vs-best-model gaps? Would the "4th" rank survive?
2. How many annotators did each language have? Could you add a second annotator and a larger sample for the key claims (Arabic STS22, multilingual sentiment)?
3. How were human clustering outputs elicited (was k given? were label names given?), and how were reranking orderings derived from binary judgments? How does the human protocol differ from the MTEB protocol (probe training for classification)? Would a few-shot-trained human or a zero-shot LLM baseline be a fairer comparison?
4. What evidence supports "models reproduce arbitrary labeling patterns"? For example, compare model agreement with human-consensus labels versus gold labels on items where humans disagree, or check overlap between these datasets and model training data.
5. How do you distinguish low κ from the effect of class imbalance and small n, given two annotators and at most 48 items? Why are κ=0.39 and κ=0.55 treated as different quality regimes?
6. Please give the number of items per cell in Table 2. Does the human rank change under a macro-average over datasets or over categories?
7. Does the model ranking on the human-annotated subset correlate with the full MTEB ranking? If the downsampling changes rankings, the subset may be biased.

## Soundness: 2
The goal is sensible, but the sample sizes are tiny, there are no CIs, many results rest on one annotator, the human and model protocols differ materially, and the main interpretive claims are asserted rather than tested.

## Presentation: 2
The paper is readable and well organised. However, protocol details are missing from the main text, some statistics are inconsistent (5 of 14 vs 26 pairs; win-rate definitions), and the abstract, discussion and conclusion state conclusions that the evidence does not carry. The figures could not be evaluated.

## Contribution: 2
The question is worthwhile and the released data could be useful to the MTEB community, but the study is small and mostly descriptive, with limited methodological or scientific insight.

## Rating: 4
Below the acceptance threshold, though not far from it. A version with a larger annotator pool, confidence intervals, controlled protocols and tested hypotheses about label noise could be a good contribution.

## Confidence: 4
