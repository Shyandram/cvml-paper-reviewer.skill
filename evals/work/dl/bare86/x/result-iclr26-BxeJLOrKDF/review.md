# Review: Mitty: Diffusion-based Human-to-Robot Video Generation (ICLR 2026)

Note: figures were not available. I judged figure-dependent claims from captions and text only.

## Summary
The paper presents Mitty, a LoRA-finetuned Wan 2.2 video diffusion transformer (5B and 14B). It translates a human demonstration video into a robot-arm execution video. The human video is VAE-encoded into clean condition tokens and concatenated with noisy robot tokens, with "bidirectional attention" between them. The paper describes this as video in-context learning. Two modes are supported: zero-frame (H2R) and first-robot-frame-conditioned (HI2R).

To address paired-data scarcity, the authors reuse the Masquerade pipeline: HaMeR hand pose, SAM2 segmentation, E2FGVI inpainting, and robot rendering. They apply it to EPIC-Kitchens, then filter by hand, which gives about 34.8k clips. They also use the Human2Robot dataset (11.8k clips). They report FVD/PSNR/SSIM/MSE, an expert-judged "task success rate" (SR), and human preference. Mitty reaches 84.5% SR, against 31.5% for Masquerade and 69–78% for Kling, MoCha and Aleph.

## Strengths
- The problem is well motivated. Replacing multi-stage pipelines (keypoints, inpainting, rendering) with a single video-to-video generator avoids compounding errors, and this is a sensible direction.
- Reusing a strong pretrained video backbone with LoRA is practical and cheap. It trains on two GPUs.
- The ablations on the reference human video are informative. Removing it hurts, especially on EPIC-Kitchens. Removing the text prompt has little effect, so the model really uses the visual demonstration.
- Qualitative comparisons with commercial video editors (Fig. 6) show that single-reference-image editing cannot keep the robot arm's structure stable. This is a useful observation, even if the baselines are not tailored to the task.
- The authors state plainly that no closed-loop or physical evaluation was done.

## Weaknesses
1. **Novelty is limited and overclaimed.** The architecture is standard in-context conditioning: concatenate clean condition tokens with noisy target tokens, as in In-Context LoRA, VACE and several of the authors' own earlier works. Eq. 4 describes "bidirectional attention" as a cross-attention-like exchange, but the paper never says how it is implemented inside Wan's DiT. Figure 2 shows ordinary self-attention with LoRA on the QKV projections. If the clean tokens simply sit in the joint self-attention sequence, the equations are misleading. Training details are also inconsistent: Eq. 5 uses ε-prediction DDPM sampling, while Wan 2.2 uses flow matching. There is no ablation comparing this attention design with plain concatenation. The "first end-to-end Human2Robot video generation" claim is not backed by a thorough related-work comparison.
2. **The ground truth for EPIC-Kitchens is the output of the pipeline the paper criticises.** The "GT" robot videos there are Masquerade renderings, filtered by humans. All FVD/PSNR/SSIM numbers on EPIC therefore measure how well Mitty imitates a synthetic renderer, not the quality of real robot execution. Training on this data effectively distils the multi-stage pipeline, and its limitations (kinematic mapping of a hand to a parallel gripper, rendering artifacts) are inherited. The pipeline still uses hand keypoints and IK to produce supervision, so the intermediate representation is bypassed only at inference. The Masquerade comparison (Sec. 4.7, Table 3) is also unfair or unclear. Mitty is trained on human-filtered outputs of Masquerade, while the Masquerade baseline appears to be unfiltered. The authors say the comparison is "not aimed at competing", yet Table 3 reports SR 31.5 vs 84.5 as a headline result. The video-editing baselines receive a different input (a single reference image, plus a mask for Kling and MoCha), and none of them is fine-tuned on paired data.
3. **The primary metric is subjective and weakly specified.** "Task success" is judged on the generated video by three domain experts. Their identity is unspecified, and so is whether they are blind to the method. The criteria are only "obvious artifacts, discontinuities, or incorrect robot motion". It is unclear how many test clips the baselines were evaluated on, and no confidence intervals or inter-rater agreement are given. A plausible-looking video is not a successful robot execution. The numbers cannot be read as task success in a physical sense, yet the abstract and Fig. 1 say Mitty "achieves high task success rates". The human-preference study has only 20 respondents, and the number of items is not stated. Embodiment Consistency is a "normalized" mix of CLIP score, DreamSim and human evaluation, and the formula is not given.
4. **Results are inconsistent with the narrative, and some are noisy.** The abstract and contribution 3 say mixed training "markedly enhances" generalization, and the conclusion says the ablations highlight the effectiveness of mixed training. Table 2 shows the opposite on Human2Robot. Mixed training drops SR from 91 to 72, PSNR from 21.7 to 16.6 and SSIM from 0.837 to 0.742. On EPIC-Seen it is also worse (88 → 81 SR). It is better only on EPIC-Unseen (86 vs 81 SR). The text then says separate training "outperforms mixed training". There is little evidence for cross-environment generalization: EPIC "unseen" is only 100 clips from other scenes in the same dataset. The Table 1 differences are small (first-frame conditioning changes PSNR by 0.2 dB, and on EPIC-Unseen SSIM even drops). With 200–500 test clips and a single run, many differences are within noise, and there are no seeds or error bars. The 14B model is labeled "T2V 14B" in Table 1 but "TI2V-14B MoE" in the setup, and it is trained for 10k steps against 20k for the 5B model, so the scaling claim is confounded. The FVD values (6–12) are very small for FVD, and the feature extractor and clip protocol are not specified.
5. **"Generalization to unseen tasks" is not evaluated.** The Human2Robot dataset has nine tasks. It is not stated whether test clips come from held-out tasks or only held-out clips of the same tasks, nor whether the split is by trajectory or by scene, which matters for near-duplicate leakage. The 41-frame, 416×224, 8 fps clips are very short and low resolution. The visual examples (Fig. 4) show simple tabletop manipulation. The robot is a single rendered arm type (RobotSuite). There is no test of a different embodiment, of real robot data from other labs, or of out-of-distribution human videos.
6. **No path to the stated motivation.** The paper motivates the work through robot learning from human demonstrations, but never shows that the generated videos can be turned into actions (e.g., via an inverse dynamics model), nor that the videos are physically feasible. There is no downstream policy experiment, even in simulation. The Limitations section is honest but admits this key gap. As it stands, this is a conditional video generation paper evaluated by visual plausibility.
7. **Missing baselines and ablations.** There is no trained baseline of the same model class: for example, a keypoint- or trajectory-conditioned video diffusion model trained on the same data. The paper's central claim is that intermediate representations lose information, and this is never tested directly. There are no ablations on the data-filtering effect (how much of the gain comes from human filtering vs the architecture), on data scale, on LoRA rank, or on the attention design. The yield and cost of human-in-the-loop filtering are not reported. The curated dataset size is inconsistent: the Fig. 3 caption says "over 6,000" pairs, while Sec. 4.2 says 34,820 clips.
8. **Presentation and reproducibility.** Section naming is confusing: "Quantitative Evaluation" refers to a qualitative figure and "Qualitative Results" to a table. There are typos ("Datasets ad Benchmark"). The code and data release is unclear (only a project page is mentioned). The curated dataset, if released, would be the most valuable contribution.

## Questions
1. How exactly is the "bidirectional attention" implemented in Wan 2.2 (joint self-attention over concatenated tokens, or an added cross-attention)? How does it differ from simple token concatenation, and is there an ablation?
2. Is Wan 2.2's flow-matching objective used, or the DDPM ε-prediction of Eqs. 1–5?
3. Who are the three experts, and are they blind to the method? What are the success criteria and the inter-rater agreement? How many clips did each baseline get? Please give confidence intervals for SR.
4. Is the Masquerade baseline in Table 3 evaluated on unfiltered outputs? What is the SR of the filtered Masquerade outputs themselves (the training-data upper bound), and of Mitty trained on unfiltered data?
5. Why does mixed training hurt so much on Human2Robot (SR 72 vs 91), and how does this square with the generalization claim? Is the 14B comparison run under equal compute?
6. How are the Human2Robot and EPIC splits constructed (by task, trajectory or scene)? Are any held-out tasks truly unseen?
7. Can actions be recovered from the generated videos, and do they match the input hand trajectories? Is there any simulation rollout?
8. How does Mitty compare with a keypoint-conditioned model trained on the same data?

## Overall assessment
The direction is reasonable and the system is cheap and practical. However, the technical novelty is thin and the central evidence is weak. The EPIC ground truth is itself synthetic and produced by the pipeline being criticised, and the main metric is a subjective video-plausibility judgement with no stated blinding. The headline Masquerade comparison is not apples-to-apples. The mixed-training claim is contradicted by the authors' own table, and there is no downstream or physical validation of the robot-learning motivation. I lean toward rejection in the current form. A controlled comparison against a keypoint-conditioned model on the same data, an objective or physical evaluation, careful held-out splits, and a clearer description of the architecture would make this much stronger.
