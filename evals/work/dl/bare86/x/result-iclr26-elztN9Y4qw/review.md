# Review: Visual Autoregressive Modeling for Instruction-Guided Image Editing (VAREdit)

Note: only the extracted text was available. Figures (attention heatmaps, radar charts, qualitative comparisons) were judged from captions and text only.

## Summary
The paper fine-tunes the pre-trained text-to-image VAR model Infinity (2.2B and 8.4B) for instruction-based image editing. The model predicts multi-scale residuals of the target image, conditioned on source-image tokens and the instruction. Two source-conditioning strategies are compared. "Full" prepends all source scales. "Finest" prepends only the finest-scale source tokens. The authors argue that Finest suffers from a scale mismatch, because fine-scale source features are used to predict coarse target scales. From one example's attention heatmaps (Fig. 3) of a Full-trained model, they conclude that only the first self-attention layer needs scale-matched source information. They propose Scale-Aligned Reference (SAR): in the first self-attention layer only, they add keys and values from a downsampled copy of the finest source feature, matched to the current target scale. Trained on 3.92M pairs (SEED-Data-Edit and ImgEdit), VAREdit reports higher GPT-4o-judged "GPT-Balance" than InstructPix2Pix, UltraEdit, OmniGen, AnySD, EditAR, ACE++ and ICEdit on EMU-Edit and PIE-Bench. It also reports 0.7–1.2 s per 512px edit, against 2.6–45 s for the baselines.

## Strengths
- It is a timely, reasonable application of next-scale prediction to editing, and the AR-vs-diffusion framing is interesting. The inference speed is a real practical advantage and is measured for all methods.
- The Full vs. Finest vs. SAR design space is cleanly stated. The SAR modification is small, cheap (same speed as Finest in Table 2), and easy to reproduce. Full conditioning is shown to be both slower (about 60%) and worse in GPT-Bal.
- Evaluation covers two benchmarks, CLIP metrics plus GPT-based metrics, per-category breakdowns, and two model sizes. The authors acknowledge the weaknesses of the 2.2B model (text and global edits) and the metric degeneracy (a model that outputs the input scores high on Over.).
- The paper is clearly written and short, and the method is easy to follow.

## Weaknesses
1. **The central claim (AR avoids the spurious-edit/entanglement problem of diffusion) is not supported.** The abstract and introduction motivate the work by diffusion models' global denoising causing unintended modifications, and by AR's causal, compositional nature solving this. However, GPT-Over. in Table 1 does not show VAREdit as superior at preservation. OmniGen is higher on both benchmarks (8.83 vs 7.52 on EMU; 9.01 vs 7.49 on PIE). UltraEdit is higher on PIE (8.35 vs 7.49 for 8.4B). AnySD is higher on EMU. The gain comes almost entirely from GPT-Suc. (about 6–7.5 vs 3–5). Nothing in the experiments isolates "locality" or "preservation" — for example, masked pixel-level or LPIPS/L1 error outside the edit region, or a controlled AR-vs-diffusion comparison with the same data and backbone. The data and compute differences below are confounders for any AR-vs-diffusion conclusion.
2. **Confounded comparison and baseline selection.** VAREdit is trained on 3.92M pairs, including ImgEdit (a recent, high-quality, GPT-4o-derived dataset), with VLM filtering and 26k iterations at batch size 1536 for the 8.4B model. Baselines use their released checkpoints trained on different, often noisier, data (e.g., InstructPix2Pix, AnySD). The baselines are also older and weak: ACE++ and AnySD score very low, and several are 2024 models. No recent strong editors appear (e.g., Step1X-Edit, BAGEL, OmniGen2, FLUX.1 Kontext, and UltraEdit/ICEdit retrained on the same data). A fair test would fine-tune a diffusion/flow model of comparable size on the identical 3.92M set. Without this, "VAR beats diffusion" is really "this dataset + a strong base model beats older released checkpoints."
3. **Metric concerns.** The primary metric, GPT-Balance, is the paper's own construction (per-sample harmonic mean of GPT-4o scores). The evaluation uses a single GPT-4o judge run with no variance, no repeated runs and no human study. Some per-sample scores in Fig. 5 are exact 0s and 10s, so the metric is highly quantised and sensitive to judge idiosyncrasies. No human evaluation or agreement with GPT scores is given. Comparisons are also affected by the resolution each model natively supports (the baselines are run at their defaults), and it is not stated whether outputs were resized for the judge. The "30%+ / 41.5% higher" headline is a relative gain on this custom metric. On the standard CLIP metrics, the gains are small or absent (EMU CLIP-Out: 0.276 vs 0.278 UltraEdit; PIE CLIP-Whole 0.255 for 8.4B vs 0.260 for 2.2B), and the text only claims "competitive or leading." The GPT judge may also favor outputs resembling GPT-4o-generated training data (ImgEdit).
4. **The SAR evidence is thin.** (a) The motivating analysis rests on one example's attention heatmap from one Full-trained model (Fig. 3). Attention patterns do not establish that scale-aligned information is *needed* in layer 1 only. There is no ablation of applying SAR in other layers, in the first k layers, or in all layers, and no comparison with simple alternatives (e.g., multi-scale source features added through a single-layer cross-attention, feature-wise addition, or concatenating all scales in the first layer only). (b) The ablation (Table 2) is only at 256px with the 2.2B model, a single seed, and gains are modest: PIE Bal. 6.588 → 6.684, and PIE Suc. actually drops from 7.291 to 7.234. Gains come mostly from Over. No error bars are given, so significance is unknown. (c) The "scale mismatch" explanation is not verified — e.g., it is untested whether the Finest-model's coarse-scale predictions are worse, or whether the improvement results from more tokens attending in layer 1. A control that provides a non-scale-aligned extra reference in layer 1 is missing. (d) Claims in the abstract ("finest-scale source features cannot effectively guide coarser target features") are stronger than the evidence.
5. **Efficiency claims need qualification.** The speed advantage compares a 512px VAR (with a small number of scales) against diffusion models run with their default step counts and resolutions, with timing hardware and batch settings not described in the main text. The 2.2× over UltraEdit is a modest gain for a model of similar size, and the 256px/512px variants are not the same operating point as the baselines. Memory and token counts are not reported. No comparison to distilled or few-step diffusion editors. EditAR's 45.5 s looks like an unoptimised implementation.
6. **Limited analysis of failures and scaling.** The 2.2B model is weak on text and global style edits, and the 8.4B model recovers (text: 2.7 → 6.3). This is attributed to scale, but the 8.4B model also uses a different codebook dimension (56 vs 32) and a different training schedule (26k at 512px vs 8k+7k), so model size is confounded with tokenizer capacity and training length. The "excellent scaling properties" claim is therefore not supported. There is no discussion of limitations such as resolution (≤512px), tokenizer reconstruction loss on preserved regions, and multi-turn edits. Reconstruction error of the BSQ tokenizer on untouched regions is a direct concern for a claim about preservation, and it is not measured.
7. **Novelty is moderate.** The pipeline is Infinity fine-tuned with source tokens prepended (similar to in-context conditioning in diffusion transformers and EditAR), and the new technical component is one additional first-layer key/value set. Training-free VAR editing (Wang et al. 2025) is cited but not compared. The data recipe (VLM filtering with Kimi-VL) is described but its effect is not ablated. Reproducibility depends on release of the models (a link is promised); the filtered training data is not described as released.

## Questions for the authors
1. Can you provide a controlled AR-vs-diffusion comparison with the same data, size and training budget (e.g., fine-tuning an SD3/FLUX-class editor on the same 3.92M pairs)? Alternatively, retrain UltraEdit/ICEdit on your data.
2. Can you report a direct preservation metric (masked L1/LPIPS/PSNR outside the edit region on PIE-Bench, which has masks) and tokenizer round-trip error, to support the claim about avoiding spurious modifications? Why is GPT-Over. lower than OmniGen/UltraEdit in several settings?
3. What are the variances across seeds and across GPT-4o judge runs for Table 1 and Table 2? Is the SAR vs Finest difference significant? Please also run the ablation at 512px with the 8.4B model.
4. Can you ablate where SAR is applied (layer 1, layers 1–k, all layers), and include a non-scale-aligned extra-reference control, to show that scale alignment (rather than extra capacity) is what matters?
5. Does the Fig. 3 attention pattern hold across many examples (average attention by layer and scale)? Why is the first layer the only one where cross-scale information matters, given that it was analysed in a model trained with Full conditioning?
6. Please add comparisons with more recent editors (Step1X-Edit, BAGEL, OmniGen2, FLUX Kontext) and human preference results. How does the GPT metric correlate with human judgement?
7. What is the effect of the VLM filtering and of the ImgEdit data on the results? Is the result sensitive to the ~1M samples discarded?
8. Which hardware, precision and sampling settings were used for the timing comparison? What are the timings at matched resolution?

## Ratings
- Soundness: 2 (promising results, but key claims about AR-vs-diffusion, scale mismatch and scaling are insufficiently controlled)
- Presentation: 3
- Contribution: 2

**Rating: 6** (borderline, leaning accept: a clear practical result and speed advantage, with a simple method, but the comparisons are confounded and the mechanistic claims are weakly supported. Would move to 8 with a controlled same-data diffusion baseline, stronger baselines and a more thorough SAR ablation.)
