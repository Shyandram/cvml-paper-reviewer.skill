# Review: EduDial: Constructing a Large-scale Multi-turn Teacher–Student Dialogue Corpus

## Summary
The paper presents EduDial, a synthetic multi-turn teacher–student dialogue dataset for K-12 mathematics. It covers 345 knowledge points in 173 chapters. Dialogues follow a five-stage teaching flow mapped to Bloom's taxonomy, with ten questioning strategies and three simulated student profiles (excellent, medium, struggling). The data comes in two parts.
- **MTI:** 13,700 SFT dialogues generated by o1, filtered by "expert-machine" verification.
- **PDTS:** 20,550 DPO preference pairs. The rejected response comes from the SFT teacher and the chosen response comes from an LLM conditioned on the intended strategy.

The authors fine-tune QwQ-32B-Preview with SFT followed by DPO to get EduDial-LLM. They also propose an 11-dimension evaluation rubric, scored by 5 experts and by GPT-4o. They compare against 17 other LLMs in simulated sessions and report that EduDial-LLM scores highest on average. Ablations show that SFT and DPO each help. A mathematical reasoning check shows a drop on Math500 and AIME24.

## Strengths
- The topic is relevant. Pedagogically grounded multi-turn tutoring data is scarce, and the design makes the teaching process explicit through stages, strategies and student profiles.
- The pipeline is described reasonably clearly. The split is by chapter, which avoids leakage of knowledge points between train and test.
- The evaluation compares many strong baselines and uses both human and LLM raters. The ablation covers base, SFT-only, DPO-only and full models, split by student type.
- The paper reports the cost of the Math500/AIME degradation instead of hiding it.
- The authors promise to release data, code and weights.

## Weaknesses
1. **Claims contradicted by the paper's own tables, and internal inconsistencies.**
   - The abstract and conclusion say EduDial-LLM is "consistently outperforming all baselines across all metrics." Table 2 contradicts this.
     - On Response (RES), EduDial-LLM scores 4.12 (M) / 4.26 (H). Claude-3.7-Thinking scores 4.63 and DeepSeek-R1 scores 4.50.
     - On Fluency (FLU), Claude-3.7-Thinking scores 4.63 against 4.43.
     - On Coverage (COV), EduDial-LLM scores 91.83 / 88.29. Gemini-2.0-Flash scores 96.16 / 94.84 and DeepSeek-V3 scores 95.04 / 94.32.
     - Relevance and the average are only comparable to the best baselines.
   - The text says content-quality scores "match the best baseline", but the table shows them below it.
   - The abstract and conclusion say 17 LLMs. Section 4.1 says 18 baselines (nine closed-source, eight open-source and EduDial-LLM itself). The counts do not agree.
   - The headline "34,250 dialogue sessions" equals 13,700 + 20,550. The 20,550 PDTS items are preference pairs over single instructions, not sessions, so the headline count is inflated.
   - Table 3 "complete model" scores per student type (e.g. INS about 4.23–4.28) do not match the Table 2 EduDial-LLM row (INS 4.58 machine / 4.43 human). The two tables do not appear to come from the same runs, and the text extraction of Table 3 is also hard to align.
2. **Circular and biased evaluation.**
   - The training data is generated by o1 under principles that mirror the evaluation dimensions (stage integrity, questioning strategies, adaptation to cognitive level, interaction). The evaluation then rewards those features, such as Interactivity, Thinking, Adaptability and Insight. The paper does not say whether baselines get the five-stage scaffold or student profiles. Without that, the comparison may measure adherence to the authors' pedagogical script, not teaching quality.
   - GPT-4o is both a data verifier/filter and the primary evaluator, so the training data was selected to please GPT-4o.
   - Chosen DPO responses are produced by an LLM given the exact target strategy. The test-time students are simulated by models fine-tuned on the same data distribution, so the setting is in-distribution for EduDial-LLM and not for the baselines.
   - There is no evaluation with real students or teachers and no measure of learning gain. High scores on "Goal" and "Thinking" are LLM-judge impressions.
   - Only 36 scenarios per model are used. There are no confidence intervals, seeds or significance tests, yet the text uses "significantly" repeatedly.
3. **Machine–human agreement is weaker than claimed.**
   - The reported Pearson/Spearman of about 0.90 is computed over 18 model-level means. That tests the ranking of models, which is dominated by the gap between strong and weak models. It does not test per-dialogue agreement.
   - The paper itself notes lower agreement on Interactivity, Feedback and Insight. Table 2 shows large machine–human gaps, for example Qwen-2.5-72B-Math (INS machine 2.02 vs human 3.06) and GPT-4o (EMO 3.47 vs 2.80). Despite this, machine ratings are adopted as the primary measure.
   - There is no inter-annotator agreement among the 5 experts, no expert qualifications, no blinding, and no rubric anchors.
4. **The data validation is unquantified.**
   - The "expert-machine dual verification" does not say how many experts were used, how many items were reviewed (all 34K?), what the rejection rate was, or how many regeneration cycles ran. Manual review of all items at this scale is hard to believe.
   - There is no dataset analysis beyond counts: no distribution of strategies, no diversity analysis, no mathematical error rate for o1-generated content, and no check that simulated "struggling students" look like real students' language and errors. These simulations are likely stereotyped.
5. **Training and evaluation setup concerns.**
   - Hyperparameters are "determined via grid search on the test set", and checkpoints are evaluated every 500 steps. This is test-set tuning.
   - The base model, QwQ-32B-Preview, is weak and dated. There is no baseline of the base or a stronger model prompted with the same teaching scaffold. There is also no SFT baseline on equal-size generic tutoring data.
   - DPO pairs compare an SFT-model rejected response with a chosen response from a stronger LLM that sees the target strategy. Length and style biases are not controlled for. Equations 3–5 omit the reference model and β, so they are not the standard DPO loss that Section 4.1 describes.
   - As a benchmark, the test split is small (36 chapters, 70 knowledge points). Using it requires running a specific student simulator and a GPT-4o judge, so it is not standardized.
6. **Scope and generalization.**
   - Everything is K-12 mathematics. The language of the data is not stated, and it is probably derived from a Chinese curriculum. Claims about "intelligent education" in general are unsupported. There is no cross-subject or cross-lingual test.
   - Reasoning drops by 3.6 points on Math500 and 10 points on AIME24. The paper calls this "within the expected range" without justification. For a tutor, accuracy of the mathematical content matters, and the correctness of the teacher's statements in dialogues is not measured.
7. **Novelty and related work.**
   - Bloom-based and Socratic LLM role-play tutoring data already exists (SocraticLM, multi-agent tutoring, and others). The new part is mostly the stage and strategy scheme and the student profiles. The claim that existing datasets use "rigid questioning strategies" and ignore cognitive differences is not tested.
   - Related work has no quantitative comparison with prior educational dialogue datasets. The citation of MT-Bench appears to be MT-Bench-101, and AlpacaEval is not a multi-turn benchmark.
8. **Presentation.** There are typos ("mutil-turn", "Reprodicibility"). Section 3.5 is truncated in the text ("with 100Evaluation…"). The 11 dimensions overlap (Response, Feedback, Insight, Goal), and their distinctness is not validated. The data and code were not available for assessment. Figures were unavailable, so I judged figure-dependent claims from captions and text only.

## Questions
1. Why do the abstract and conclusion claim superiority on all metrics when Table 2 shows several baselines ahead on RES, FLU and COV?
2. Which prompts were used for baselines? Do they receive the five-stage process and student profiles? How does QwQ-32B (and a stronger base) do when prompted with the same scaffold?
3. Why do the Table 3 and Table 2 numbers for the full model differ, and which student simulator and scenarios were used in each?
4. How many items did experts review, how many were rejected, and what was the dialogue-level agreement among the 5 raters and with GPT-4o?
5. Was GPT-4o's self-preference bias or bias towards o1-style data checked? Can you add a judge from a different model family?
6. Which split was used for grid search and checkpoint selection? Can you re-evaluate on a truly held-out set?
7. Can you evaluate with student simulators that are independent of your data, with real students, or with a learning-outcome measure?
8. What is the factual and mathematical error rate in the o1-generated dialogues and in EduDial-LLM outputs?

## Ratings
- Soundness: 2/4
- Presentation: 2/4
- Contribution: 2/4
- Rating: 4 (marginally below the acceptance threshold)
- Confidence: 4/5
